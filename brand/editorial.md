# Verbal identity and editorial style

Saint Paul After Dark · Owner review edition · November 12, 2026

The tour speaks as a local historian who can tell a story and show their work. Use the city’s places and objects to open a question, then give the reader enough context to weigh the answer. Write about the people who carried the consequences as carefully as the people who gained from the arrangement.

## Name, signature, and point of view

| Where | Language |
| --- | --- |
| Project title | Saint Paul After Dark |
| Outside the tour | A digital history tour of Saint Paul’s underworld, 1900–1938 |
| Signature line | Every city has rules. Saint Paul had an arrangement. |
| Main visitor invitation | Enter the story |
| Narrative point of view | Third person for historical action. Second person for a reader action or a question. |
| Owner notes and invitations | First person, with a concrete request and enough context to answer. |

Use the full name on first mention. In continuous prose use “the tour” or “this edition” for later references. Use **Saint Paul** for the city. Preserve the spelling of an organization, source title, or quotation. Write the navigation names as displayed, such as **Casebook**, **Sources**, and **Calm view**. Style a chapter name in quotation marks in body copy and plain text in its own heading.

The opening can be tense. The evidence is precise. The controls are direct. Keep the historical narrator free of period slang and first-person reenactment. When a voice comes from a record, use attribution and identify the type of source.

## Tone by surface

| Surface | What the voice should do | Sample |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cover | Give one reason to enter and identify the format. | Every city has rules. Saint Paul had an arrangement. |
| Chapter opening | Put a place, object, or decision in front of the reader and ask one question. | Who sat here? |
| Chapter reading | State the event, name the source where needed, and explain who could act or who bore the cost. | The investigators’ summary reports that Walter Magee and the Bremer family notified authorities. |
| Evidence panel | Name the material and state what it supports with its qualification beside it. | A 1933 business notice gives Castle Royal’s proposed business purposes. Its scope is the business. The patron stories come from other accounts. |
| Map and directory | Help readers locate the chapter and understand the precision of the map. | The directory includes all seven stops. The map shows approximate positions for three venues. |
| Method or correction | Put the research action first. Keep the wording calm and traceable. | Follow the chapter’s claim ID to the source and its passage locator. |
| Controls and feedback | Name the action or report a state and the next available step. | Play narration. Read the introduction. |
| Outreach | State what it is, why it fits the recipient, and where to look. | Here’s a seven-chapter history tour people can take from home. “Under the Bluff” is a short entry point. |

## Terms with historical weight

| Use | When it fits |
| --- | --- |
| **Protection arrangement**, **the arrangement**, **layover system** | Describe the informal system. Add the terms and their source when they matter. In the opening, “arrangement” carries the signature and the core question. |
| **Police chief John O’Connor**, **family**, **juror**, **intermediary** | Introduce someone by their role in the story. Give their agency and actions the amount of detail the record can support. |
| **National Prohibition** | Refer to the legal era. Use **1900–1938** for the span of the tour, which takes in the arrangement’s beginnings and later reform. |
| **Historical record**, **later account**, **tour explanation** | Name the kind of material the reader is seeing. If a web page reproduces a record, identify the original material and the form examined. |
| **Reports**, **dates**, **attributes**, **interprets** | Keep the source attached to the action or reading it supplies. Match the verb to the type of source. |
| **Coercion**, **abduction**, **ransom demand**, **corruption** | Use the direct term supported by the record. Name the people affected where the evidence supports it. |
| **Approximate venue position** | Describe the point on the current map. Treat a venue’s present-day access, the site of a historical event, and coordinate precision as distinct questions. |
| **AI-generated illustration**, **synthesized narration**, **synthesized ambience** | Use the plain term for each medium. Put the art credit next to the image or in the caption and keep the narration text available. |

Use **September 30, 1933** in running prose and **1900–1938** for compact date spans. Give exact days only where the cited record supplies them. Keep claim IDs uppercase, such as **C12**, and show a descriptive link with the ID when context is thin. Use numerals for years, chapter numbers in navigation, and formal record references. Spell out “seven chapters” in prose.

## How to write the claim and its limits

The [evidence ledger](../docs/evidence-ledger.md) defines the formal statuses and review practice. Copy keeps the relationship between source, claim, explanation, and reading visible.

| Evidence condition | Wording to reach for |
| --- | --- |
| A period record supports a bounded fact | “Commercial West published a Castle Royal business notice on September 30, 1933.” Keep the claim and source accessible from the passage. |
| A later account supplies a detail | “Historian Greg Brick dates the nightclub’s opening to October 26, 1933.” Keep the historian’s role visible even in short copy. |
| Accounts differ | “Later accounts give conflicting recollections of the nightclub’s guests.” Say which accounts differ when space permits. |
| The project has yet to establish adequate support | “The sources examined here leave that guest story unverified.” State the scope of the search. |
| The passage makes an interpretation | “The tour reads the ransom demands as an effort to control how families responded.” Tie the inference to the facts and preserve their qualifications. |

An illustration, animation, or soundtrack may create a sense of place. Give visual captions enough information to identify the depiction and its role. In “Under the Bluff,” the empty chair gives readers a question. The business notice, the historian’s account, and the later recollections give them material to inspect.

Use source text with a verified passage locator and appropriate usage permission. Keep quotations exact. Preserve attribution for paraphrases. Carry a significant qualification into the caption, share text, or chapter teaser when the line could travel on its own.

## Interface language

Use sentence case and an action verb for task labels. Keep the label readable beside a glyph and use the same label in the accessible name. Prefer names that identify where a link leads, such as “Read the sources” or “Open the claim ledger.” Use chapter names for chapter links. Use “Read the introduction” for the text that serves as the audio transcript.

| State | Suggested copy |
| --- | --- |
| Start at the top | Enter the story |
| Pick another entry point | Choose a chapter |
| Return to a saved position | Resume reading |
| Hear the introduction | Play narration |
| Hear the setting | Play scene sound |
| Pause the current audio | Pause narration |
| Read the same introduction as text | Read the introduction |
| Failed narration | Narration could not play. Try again or read the introduction. |
| Local progress cannot persist | This browser can’t save your place. You can keep reading in this tab. |

Keep audio status text next to the relevant control. Announce changes briefly where the current application supports it. Name control states with visible words and programmatic state. Use text labels and identifiers alongside claim colors and glyphs. Keep any essential detail from a visual card in its caption or alt text. Describe what a photograph or illustration shows, and use the caption to carry material origin, historical qualification, and credit.

## Edit and review

1. Mark the exact copy and the route, catalog entry, or asset it belongs to.
2. Read the claim, source locator, and qualification together. For a new or revised claim, use the existing catalog process and trace every place it appears.
3. Check the same sentence in its shortest likely use, such as a share card, caption, audio intro, or chapter teaser.
4. If the correction changes a sourced statement, log the affected claim IDs and why the language changed. If it changes a stop introduction, flag its narration digest and arrange the project’s audio update and human listening review.
5. Ask the human reviewer to record their decision at the appropriate edition and asset revision. Link a published correction from the places the project already gives readers to find one.
