# Campaign and launch playbook

Saint Paul After Dark · Owner review edition · November 12, 2026

The campaign introduces an independent, at-home history tour. Local-history readers are the first audience. Remote readers and the people who recommend good local history are the next. Give each piece one link and one reason to follow it.

Lead with the descriptor in tourism listings, search copy, and partner outreach. Use the signature as a headline when the title and descriptor can travel with it. Write **Saint Paul** in running copy. Use **St. Paul** only where it is part of a proper name, a source title, or a search phrase being discussed.

The reader’s first job is to recognize a web story they can read at home. The opening chapter then poses the central question. The casebook, map, and claim ledger give curious readers ways to keep looking. The chapter art gives the campaign a memorable object or place. The body copy makes clear what the historical record can establish.

### Writing decisions

- Use people, objects, and places as entry points. Start with a chair, a business notice, a payment, or a city rule, then say what the reader can find in the story.
- Give victims and institutional responsibility room in the copy. Use direct language for coercion and harm. Treat criminal figures as people in the record and leave out nicknames used as sales hooks.
- Attribute period claims when the tour does. Keep a documented date, an attributed account, and a disputed recollection distinct. Link any chapter-specific claim to its chapter or ledger entry.
- Use “AI-generated illustration” in the asset, caption, or both wherever the image could be read as a historical view. In copy use “optional synthesized narration for chapter introductions.” Keep narration and synthesized scene ambience distinct.
- Describe the map as a directory of seven places with approximate positions for some venues. Its current base shows the modern river. For partner listings, use “digital history tour” or “at-home digital history tour.” For any separate visit, direct people to the venue for access information.
- Use the exact content note on a detail page or announcement with room for it. “Contains historical accounts of criminal extortion, abduction, armed confrontation, and institutional corruption in 1900s–1930s Saint Paul.”

Begin with the owner’s own channels and willing local-history readers. A library newsletter can use the directory copy and link preview. An editor can use the Castle Royal source comparison or the wider question of how the city’s protection arrangement held together. A venue partner can link to a relevant chapter while describing its own present-day access and programs separately. Each placement should stand on its own.

### Local outreach shortlist

These are proposed approaches based on the organizations’ own materials, checked November 12, 2026. Start with one reader fit and one relevant link per pitch.

- **Saint Paul Public Library.** The Saint Paul Collection brings together city directories, clippings, maps, and building histories. Offer the method page and one chapter as a reference example showing how a visitor can move from a place to a source. [Saint Paul Collection](https://sppl.org/saint-paul-collection/)
- **Ramsey County Historical Society.** Its publishing program covers county history, including ordinary people’s lives. Offer a short piece on how the protection arrangement shifted risk and ask whether its research method or source notes would suit an editorial conversation. [Ramsey County Historical Society publishing program](https://rchs.com/about/)
- **Landmark Center.** The former federal courthouse offers historical and architectural tours, including a virtual tour. If the staff are interested, offer “On the Record” as optional reading on court cases and institutional reform, and link visitors to the venue’s own information for an on-site visit. [Landmark Center tours](https://www.landmarkcenter.org/tours/)
- **Visit Saint Paul.** Its gangster-history page points visitors to physical and virtual tours. Offer the directory blurb for a clearly identified at-home listing and include a chapter that shows the source links. [Visit Saint Paul’s gangster-history guide](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/gangster-past/)

## Measure the reader’s next step

Use a short, consent-based reader task and a simple campaign log. The tour currently runs without analytics or accounts. Local bookmarks are a convenience for the reader and provide no campaign totals. Platform totals, where already available, describe activity on that platform.

| Question | Evidence | What to change if it repeats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can someone tell what this is before clicking? | Show a card briefly and ask what they expect. Record the number answering “online, self-guided history” and the number asked. | Move “digital history tour” closer to the title. Use the name and descriptor in the opening line. |
| Can someone begin without help? | Ask a willing reader to start and locate a chapter. Record independent starts out of attempts and where help was needed. | Make “Enter the story” easier to spot. Check the link and the first chapter’s entry. |
| Can someone find the basis for a claim? | Give a reader a chapter and ask them to find what the notice supports and where it is cited. Record where they stopped. | Link to the specific evidence item and keep the qualification beside the statement. |
| Which audience chose to follow up? | Track replies, useful source suggestions, agreed partner mentions, and public platform-level link counts where provided. Label each source and date. | Repeat placements that led to specific reading or referral feedback. |
| Is the public promise accurate? | Log questions about bookings, walking directions, archive images, or availability, and reconcile them with edition notes. | Correct the copy that prompted the question and update reusable captions and assets. |

For a first small round, log five willing-reader attempts if available. Record what happened and avoid treating the small sample as a population estimate. Keep feedback in aggregate or with the reader’s permission. Example log fields are date, edition, channel, asset, target, attempts, observed starts, source finds, correction, and next change. When an outcome is unobserved, leave it unknown.
