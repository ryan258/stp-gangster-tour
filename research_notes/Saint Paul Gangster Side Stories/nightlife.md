# Saint Paul nightlife, vice and overlooked people

## Which places offer a new story with a strong record?

### Takeaway
The Victoria Café offers the best audible period object and a concrete conflict over ginger ale. The Hollyhocks Inn offers the closest direct link to the tour’s Hamm kidnapping chapter, with an archived interview about a fugitive coming in for dinner.

### Cited Findings
- **A band with its own moonshine song.** At 825 University Avenue West, Frogtown’s former Victoria Theater became the Victoria Café. Frank Cloutier and the Victoria Cafe Orchestra made their Gennett recording of “Moonshiner’s Dance” in Saint Paul in 1927. It was recorded at the Lowry Hotel, in late September per researcher Kurt Gegenhuber, and released in January 1928. Part One appeared in Harry Smith’s 1952 *Anthology of American Folk Music*. Smithsonian Folkways’ 1997 date is for its reissue. Gegenhuber cites party favors including tiny tin saxophone noisemakers and reproduces the café’s opening ad. The city designated the building a heritage preservation site in 2011. [Smithsonian Folkways track record](https://folkways.si.edu/frank-cloutier-and-the-victoria-cafe-orchestra/moonshiners-dance-part-one/african-american-music-american-folk-blues-old-time/music/track/smithsonian), [Historic Saint Paul on the recording and building](https://www.historicsaintpaul.org/neighborhood-preservation/projects/victoria-theater), [Gegenhuber’s chapter, pp. 146–151 and 162–163](https://www.kurtgegenhuber.com/uploads/1/3/0/5/130566057/gegenhuber_moonshiner_essay.pdf), [Saint Paul ordinance](https://stpaul.legistar.com/LegislationDetail.aspx?FullText=1&GUID=7951FF83-0FF8-48B7-A988-2C847D227B24&ID=846363).
- **The café that got padlocked over mixers.** Gegenhuber traces undercover agents reporting that Victoria Café staff served ginger ale and ice to people bringing their own liquor. A federal judge closed the café for six months in March 1928. In a separate raid on December 30, 1928, former assistant Ramsey County attorney and amateur boxer Allan McGill punched a federal agent. McGill said he thought the café was being held up. He telegraphed his congressman, Melvin Maas, and federal officials promised an investigation. The chapter reproduces the *Pioneer Press* front page and McGill telegram and identifies its newspaper sources in notes 39–46. [Gegenhuber, pp. 162–165](https://www.kurtgegenhuber.com/uploads/1/3/0/5/130566057/gegenhuber_moonshiner_essay.pdf), [MPR’s account of the preservation research](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2009/12/23/victoria-theatre-historic-designation).
- **Dinner at the Hollyhocks Inn.** In an October 11, 1934 federal interview summary, proprietor Jack Peifer, spelled Pfeiffer in the report, said escaped federal prisoner Frank Nash and his wife had dinner at his Saint Paul inn around ten days before Nash’s death in the Kansas City Massacre. Peifer denied receiving a call from Verne Miller from Kansas City and denied knowledge of the killing. This is evidence of what Peifer told agents, including what he acknowledged and denied. A 1936 Associated Press dispatch records Peifer’s conviction in the Hamm kidnapping, his 30-year sentence on July 31 and his death the same day. The MNHS Maccabee collection has a Hollyhocks folder at box 5 under the historical address 1590 S. Mississippi River Blvd. The finding aid also names doorman Martin Rohling and chef Sam Tanaka as research leads. [FBI Vault, part 66, locate “On October 11, 1934 JACK PFEIFFER”](https://vault.fbi.gov/Kansas%20City%20Massacre/Kansas%20City%20Massacre%20Part%2066), [July 31, 1936 AP account in Capital Journal, front page](https://oregonnews.uoregon.edu/lccn/sn90066132/1936-07-31/ed-1/seq-1.pdf), [MNHS finding aid](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/00239.html).

### Inferences
- Victoria can branch from *Under the Bluff* through a record a visitor can listen to at the source and the story of a night out. Hollyhocks can branch from *The Price of Protection* or *On the Record* through the documented proprietor, with the interview as an attributed scene.
- Searches in the current `src/data/`, `docs/`, and README found no references to Victoria Café, Cloutier, Hollyhocks, Peifer/Pfeiffer, or Nash. The Castle Royal stop already uses a business notice and explores a disputed guest list, so the new material should introduce actual venue records and specific incidents.

### Gaps
- I did not find McGill’s case outcome. The source gives McGill’s explanation for the punch, with disputed enforcement conduct reported by patrons and staff. No tie between Victoria and a particular gangster is established by these sources. Use a source link for the recording and route any audio reproduction through the project’s rights process.
- Avoid the Hollyhocks envelope/20 percent, ghost or named guest lore until separately substantiated. The archive’s historical address does not establish a place the public can enter. News coverage differed in detail about Peifer’s death, so the conviction, sentence, and date are enough for this story.

## Which odd business details can we tell without stretching the evidence?

### Takeaway
The appeal over a West Seventh “soft drink bar” gives the clearest court record. A liquor faucet in a flower shop is the most vivid anecdote, with weaker evidence for the incident itself than for the person who told it.

### Cited Findings
- **A soft drink license and a saloon behind the bar.** In *Engler v. United States*, 25 F.2d 37 (8th Cir. 1928), the court recounted four sales of alcohol on April 6, 1925, and liquor found April 15, at 905 West Seventh Street. The affidavits described a bar, back bar, mirror and glassware like those of an ordinary saloon. The owner changed tenants, but the court noted the equipment and the business carried on. On March 16, 1928 the appellate court affirmed the order barring occupation/use of the premises for one year from the April 1927 district court decree. This was a civil nuisance proceeding concerning the premises. [Published appellate opinion, facts and ruling](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/25/37/1548515/).
- **The ferns hid a liquor faucet.** Helen Warren Pfleger, who joined Saint Paul’s Prohibition Office in 1925 as a receptionist, clerk and switchboard operator, recalled in a 1975 memoir that a federal investigator searching a Saint Paul flower shop noticed an extra faucet behind a stand of ferns. She said turning it produced liquor. She also recalled covering the night switchboard when agents were out on operations, and seeing ordinarily well-dressed agents disguise themselves in worn clothes. The memoir says Andrew Volstead worked there as a legal adviser and did not go on raids. [Helen Warren Pfleger as told to George A. Rea, Ramsey County History, Spring/Summer 1975, pp. 19–20](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Spring-Summer1975_Pfleger.pdf).

### Inferences
- The civil case gives concrete texture to the distinction between legal storefront and illegal sale. Pfleger’s switchboard makes an original viewpoint with a real job, workplace and recollections. The florist could appear as a very short attributed anecdote within that story.
- Searches of current site data and docs found no Engler, Pfleger, or flower-shop material.

### Gaps
- The Engler decision establishes the violations under Ayd and Davidson. A later tenant had not been charged according to the stipulated facts. Avoid claiming a new violation by that tenant or a conviction of the landlord, or inventing an organized-crime connection.
- Pfleger did not say she was in the flower shop. I did not find the shop address, date, proprietor’s name, enforcement disposition or independent confirmation, so it cannot support a map marker or a fully verified caper.

## Which story puts women and workers in the foreground?

### Takeaway
Nina Clifford’s brothel and its excavation offer a concrete way to tell a story about downtown vice and its workers. The archaeology is more useful than a purported secret tunnel.

### Cited Findings
- **What came out of the ground at Nina Clifford’s.** MNopedia identifies Clifford’s brothel at 147 South Washington Street, in business until her 1929 death, and describes eleven sex workers, two maids and a cook by 1895. It describes Clifford’s relationship with Ida Dorsey, who purchased a building from her at 151 South Washington Street. MNopedia cites a February 13, 1914 newspaper report of graft testimony for its coverage of ties to city police. Construction of the new Science Museum prompted a 1997 salvage excavation at the former red-light district. Archaeologists found the foundations of Clifford’s brothel and compared artifacts at the front and rear. The total of more than 14,000 objects covers multiple brothels and a saloon privy across the excavation. [MNopedia profile and bibliography](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/clifford-nina-1851-1929), [Washington Street archaeology summary and excavation photo](https://www.fromsitetostory.org/tcm/21ra0032wsrd/21ra0032wsrd.asp).
- MNopedia describes the supposed tunnel between Clifford’s brothel and the Minnesota Club as legend and reports that no tunnels were found in the excavation. It gives the story of a chandelier going to a mayoral office as an unverified account. [MNopedia, concluding biographical paragraphs](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/clifford-nina-1851-1929).

### Inferences
- A story keyed to what archaeologists could recover and to the people who lived and worked there would add an angle to *The Arrangement*. The period of Clifford’s business overlaps the start of the tour’s timeline. Searches in current data and docs found no Clifford reference.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the 1914 testimony against the period newspaper itself or recover the full artifact report. Do not imply the evidence links Clifford to famous 1930s fugitives or infer a relationship with particular patrons from the archaeology. Keep the raid, current building access and image-rights claims separate from the historical place.
