# Courtrooms, police headquarters, and the local press

## Which newly documented incidents could stand on their own?

### Takeaway

Fred Raasch’s trial supplies the strongest scene. Jurors heard recorded calls on headsets and the court had to replay them when a needle skipped. The federal letter about Charles Tierney gives a different outcome: an officer challenged a purported recording that the agent had not heard. A 1936 editorial records a glimpse of a former chief’s money at a civil service hearing, and an appellate opinion lists the fixtures of a post-repeal apartment speakeasy.

### Cited Findings

- **The jury heard the needle skip.** Fred W. Raasch, assistant to the inspector of detectives in Saint Paul, was convicted of malfeasance for helping gamblers evade the law. The case concerned the Royal Cigar Store at 443 St. Peter Street, which took horse-race bets, and the Riverview Commercial Club, which ran slot machines. The phone line serving Raasch and his superior James Crumley was known as Cedar 5328 and bypassed the police switchboard. Operators tapped it, used a device the opinion calls a pamograph, and could listen without recording when talk seemed irrelevant. The jurors and lawyers heard the recordings through headsets. When the needle jumped a groove, the recording was replayed to recover the omitted passage. The typed transcripts helped witnesses remember and the court follow the recording. The court says the transcripts were not admitted as exhibits. The Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed denial of a new trial, finding the recordings sufficiently identified and the evidence sufficient to support the jury’s finding that Raasch gave gamblers information and advice. [State v. Raasch, 201 Minn. 158, 275 N.W. 620 (Nov. 5, 1937), printed pp. 159–162, especially 160–161 on the headsets and playback](https://law.justia.com/cases/minnesota/supreme-court/1937/201-minn-158.html), [OpenJurist transcript, ¶¶2–8](https://openjurist.org/201/minn/158/state-v-raasch).
- **Play the recording or take it back.** In a confidential March letter, Special Agent in Charge O. W. Stein described John E. Brennan’s interview the previous day with assistant inspector Charles Tierney. Brennan brought up a reported recording in which Tierney was alleged to have told an unidentified person not to tell Brennan anything. Tierney denied that conversation and demanded they go to Daily News editor Howard Kahn and have him play the record or retract the claim. Brennan declined to do so. The letter reports Tierney’s argument that some calls attributed to him had been misinterpreted or distorted. It also records his pledge to cooperate with the Bureau. [Bremer Kidnapping, Part 205, March 14 “PERSONAL AND CONFIDENTIAL” letter, find “Tierney vehemently denied” and read through the O. W. Stein signature](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20205/).
- **The gold, the thousand-dollar bill, and the safe-deposit boxes.** A September 24, 1936 Saint Paul Daily News editorial says dismissed detective and former chief Thomas A. Brown admitted at a civil service hearing that he had turned in $900 in gold for currency under the federal gold-hoarding order, changed a $1,000 bill at a Saint Paul bank in 1933 or 1934, made loans, and used three safe-deposit boxes. The paper highlighted his reported monthly police salary ceiling of $158 except during his two years as chief. The editorial acknowledged the possibility of an explanation and the hostility of other witnesses. It describes the hearing as a review of his fitness for police service. [Saint Paul Daily News, September 24, 1936 editorial “BROWN HEARING,” preserved in Bremer Kidnapping Part 267](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20267).
- **Slot machines on rubber pads, and beer on ice.** A 1936 opinion says Saint Paul police went to Kaasa’s East Eleventh Street apartment and asked for “Slim.” The officers’ inventory included about ten cases of beer, some chilled, two dozen whiskey and highball glasses, and two nickel and dime slot machines with money in them, set on rubber pads. Kaasa attributed some goods to a man he said was in Winnipeg and disclaimed knowing how the slot machines got there. The trial court convicted him of keeping liquor for sale without a city license, and the Minnesota Supreme Court upheld the judgment. The opinion says police had no search warrant and no actual sale was shown. It held that the items could be used as evidence under the Minnesota decisions it applied in 1936. [State v. Kaasa, 198 Minn. 181 (Oct. 23, 1936), printed pp. 181–184, especially pp. 182–183 for the inventory and defense](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/state-v-kaasa-minn-1936).

### Inferences

- *On the Record* already describes the general wiretap investigation and resulting reforms. Raasch adds the line, machine, courtroom procedure, named betting venues, and judicial outcome. It can stand beside Tierney’s challenge, with a clear distinction between records a court heard and a reported call no one played in the episode. Neither scene is in the current Casebook or earlier reports. [Current stop data](../../src/data/stops.json), [Current Casebook data](../../src/data/casebook-stories.json), [State v. Raasch](https://law.justia.com/cases/minnesota/supreme-court/1937/201-minn-158.html), [Tierney interview letter](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20205/).
- The newspaper’s account of Brown’s assets makes a narrow, attributed hearing story. The apartment inventory adds a view of vice enforcement after national repeal and has a stronger documentary outcome than a story that merely infers a racketeering connection. [“BROWN HEARING”](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20267), [State v. Kaasa](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/state-v-kaasa-minn-1936).

### Gaps

- **Raasch:** The opinion gives November 5, 1937 as the appeal date but gives no exact dates of the recorded calls. MNopedia describes an investigation completed in July 1935, and a separate November 1935 FBI letter ties Crumley’s suspension to Commissioner Ned Warren’s investigation in cooperation with the Daily News. These establish context, not the precise date of any Raasch recording. The opinion names Jamie as the person in charge of the wiretapping. Keep responsibility and individual recording dates within what the sources say. The physical recording and original transcript were not examined. [Police Department Reform](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/st-paul-police-department-reform-1933-1940), [November 25, 1935 FBI letter on James P. Crumley, file stamp 7-576-8170](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20173), [State v. Raasch](https://openjurist.org/201/minn/158/state-v-raasch).
- **Tierney:** The letter is primary evidence of a challenge to an allegation. It does not establish a tap of Tierney’s line, a recording’s existence or contents, or a finding against Tierney. The sender reports what his colleague Brennan and Tierney discussed. Avoid repeating unrelated accusations in the same letter as established. Omit the exact year in visitor copy until the scan can be checked visually. The text extraction gives 1935, but the letter discusses the completed grand jury investigation and sits between March 1936 records. Use “a March letter in the Bremer files.”
- **Brown:** The source is the paper’s contemporary editorial on testimony. The board’s signed findings and bank records were not examined. The figures do not establish the source of Brown’s money or any financial crime. Brown’s 1934 role in the Van Meter shooting and the 1932 raid on the Barker-Karpis rental are already adjacent to other Casebook stories and are outside this hearing vignette.
- **Kaasa:** The opinion establishes the police account and the liquor-license conviction. It does not establish a slot-machine conviction or a named gang connection. The case has a corrupted “Mich.” in the host’s citation widget. Use the opinion heading and citation 198 Minn. 181, 269 N.W. 365, Minnesota Supreme Court.
- **Reserve, inventory only:** A December 30, 1935 request from local reporter Louis H. Gollop asks J. Edgar Hoover to list the guns recovered from Bremer kidnapping suspects and identify where and from whom they were taken. Hoover’s January 11, 1936 reply names protective vests and extra ammunition drums as well as weapons and says a photo of the arsenal recovered where Ma and Fred Barker died near Okeechobee was enclosed. That separately itemized haul included two Thompsons, two twelve-gauge shotguns and a .33-caliber Winchester. It is a press correspondence story. The total varies within the PDF transcription and the overall list does not attach each gun to a person. An enclosure photo and resulting newspaper publication were not verified. [Bremer Kidnapping, Part 183, Gollop’s December 30, 1935 letter and Hoover’s January 11, 1936 two-page reply](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20183/at_download/file).
