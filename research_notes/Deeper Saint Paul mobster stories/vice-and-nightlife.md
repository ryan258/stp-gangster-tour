# Objects in Saint Paul vice records

## What did a grocery owner's keys open?

### Takeaway
A 1923 federal opinion preserves a Saint Paul storefront, a back courtyard, keys presented as car keys, and a couch with moonshine underneath. The court distinguishes what agents testified from what the defense contested.

### Cited Findings
- On March 13, 1923, two federal Prohibition agents, a man apparently acting as an informer, and a man named Kelly entered what appeared to be a grocery, candy and cigar store at 301 East Thirteenth Street in Saint Paul. Agents testified that Berman went through the back door, returned with moonshine, sold four drinks at 25 cents each and a bottle for $1, and handed a $5 bill to Weinberg, who rang up the transaction. On the second visit, they still could not see which direction Berman took when he shut the courtyard door. [*United States v. Weinberg*, 293 F. 415 (D. Minn. 1923), printed pp. 415–416, opening paragraphs](https://hallapproved.com/mn/cases/federal/district/1923/8847590/).
- On April 24, according to the opinion’s account of the search, Weinberg produced two Yale keys and said they were for his car. An agent tried a key in a locked door off the courtyard and it opened. Agents reported finding moonshine in five-gallon jugs, bottles under the couch, a bag of corks, and two funnels hanging above the couch. Both defendants denied the agents’ account, and the defense described the searched room as part of a residence. The opinion records guilty verdicts on the possession and sale counts discussed and denies the application for a writ of error. [*Weinberg*, printed pp. 416–417, especially “he said were keys for his automobile,” and concluding disposition](https://hallapproved.com/mn/cases/federal/district/1923/8847590/).

### Inferences
- The cash register, the courtyard door, and the couch give the episode three clear beats. This is a verified local Prohibition case. Current Casebook stories cover the Eddie Green keys in a different investigation and a café prosecuted over mixers, without this incident.

### Gaps
- I found no verified link from Weinberg or Berman to a named gang. Avoid claiming a specific conviction sentence or deciding the underlying scope of the search warrant. The opinion deals with the application for a writ of error and notes missing documents and insufficiently preserved objections.

## Which beer bottle sent investigators around the neighborhood?

### Takeaway
The federal file preserves the brand of beer and the shop-to-shop search, an investigative dead end distinct from the Nash dinner story and the gasoline-can fingerprint.

### Cited Findings
- Saint Paul police recovered a bottle from 804 Vernon Avenue, Saint Paul, in June 1933. In a January 31, 1934 letter, Special Agent in Charge John Hanni quotes a June 23, 1933 Western Union telegram matching the latent print to Frank Nash’s right thumb. The letter also shows Hanni trying to reconstruct whether he told the city police about that identification. It cites contemporary newspapers for the tip and delayed inspection chronology. [FBI Vault, Kansas City Massacre Part 37, Hanni to Director, Jan. 31, 1934, file 62-888, search “Latent fingerprints submitted by St Paul Police”](https://vault.fbi.gov/Kansas%20City%20Massacre/Kansas%20City%20Massacre%20Part%2037/at_download/file).
- An investigative report records six bottles of Squeezor beer and the attempt to find the shop that sold them. It says the agents interviewed shopkeepers, showed photographs, and identified two nearby businesses carrying that brand, Kregel & Decker and the St. Clair Street Shop. They never found the purchase location in the documented search. The local police identification chief and police chief told the agent that Nash’s bottle was the only examined one with fingerprints, and the report says Saint Paul police were preserving it. [FBI Vault, Kansas City Massacre Part 13, internal p. 14, paragraphs starting “Considerable time was spent interviewing shop keepers” and “At the St. Clair Street shop”](https://vault.fbi.gov/Kansas%20City%20Massacre/Kansas%20City%20Massacre%20Part%2013).

### Inferences
- Center the bottle and the shopkeepers. The established record gives the artifact, place, identification and fruitless follow-up. The current Hollyhocks story covers Peifer’s recollection of Nash at dinner and has no Vernon Avenue or bottle material. The physical trace here was a beer bottle investigated by police and federal agents in 1933, distinct from the later gasoline-can case.

### Gaps
- A print does not place Nash in the house at a specific time or establish where he bought or drank the beer. The telephone notification and timing of the police visit in Hanni’s letter are uncertain or attributed to other records. No causal role in the Hamm kidnapping can be inferred from the object itself.

## What did Mystic Caverns promise under the High Bridge?

### Takeaway
A reproduced 1933 advertisement and an archival finding aid offer a separate cave story. The objects are a neon skull and a ballroom advertised with two thousand mirrors.

### Cited Findings
- Greg Brick reproduces an April 14, 1933 *St. Paul Daily News* advertisement for Mystic Caverns and describes period ads promising the Silver Cave, Jack Foster’s Ten Cavemen and a “Rainbow Shower of 2,000 Mirrors.” The directions told drivers to follow the river road beneath the High Bridge to a neon skull and crossbones. Brick identifies co-owner Foster as the Saint Paul police band leader and says a Ramsey County grand jury investigation into gambling led to closure in 1934. [Greg Brick, *Subterranean Twin Cities*, chapter “The Medieval Temples of Mushroom Valley,” printed pp. 113–114, illustrated ad and adjoining paragraphs, author-uploaded chapter](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386279641_Chapter_Title_THE_MEDIEVAL_TEMPLES_OF_MUSHROOM_VALLEY_ST_PAUL%27S_WEST_SIDE_Book_Title_Subterranean_Twin_Cities), [publisher’s JSTOR chapter record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.5749/j.ctttv7dz.11).
- Ray Barton remembers exploring the dark abandoned cave as a boy. He later checked city directories and found it listed as a restaurant at 676 Joy Avenue. The Minnesota Historical Society’s Maccabee collection lists Robert “Frisco Dutch” Steinhardt as a casino operator at Mystic Caverns. [Barton, “Mystic Caverns and Their Short-lived Glory Days,” *Ramsey County History*, Spring 2000, printed p. 21 and map](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Spring2000_Growing_Up.pdf), [MNHS Maccabee collection, entry for Steinhardt](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/00239.html).

### Inferences
- Present the advertisement’s language as what the club wanted people to picture. The police-band connection and the archival casino entry make it more than another guest-list story. The existing Wabasha chapter concerns Castle Royal, a different nightclub.

### Gaps
- The advertising does not prove the mirror count or that every advertised spectacle took place. I did not inspect the gambling case file or verify specific convictions, guest names, or the present state of the site. The JSTOR chapter did not open through the research tool, but the author-uploaded copy did.

## What happened to the man in hospital slippers?

### Takeaway
Paul R. Gold links a Christmas Eve apartment shooting to a January hospital escape in pajamas. A clipping reproduced in his article directly supports Taran’s shooting and its address. The stairs and slippers are Gold’s researched account.

### Cited Findings
- A contemporary clipping reproduced by Gold says former boxer Sam Terrin, also known as Sam Taran, was wounded when two men attacked him at his apartment at 558 St. Peter Street. Gold dates the attack to Christmas Eve 1925, names Harry Carlin and William “Billy” Alexander, and describes wounded Taran disarming the attackers and throwing Alexander down a staircase. Alexander was taken to Ancker Hospital. Gold reports that at Carlin’s later trial Taran said he did not remember the attackers or recognize the two guns shown to him. Carlin blamed Alexander for shooting Taran. [Gold, “The Legend of Sam Taran,” *Ramsey County History*, Winter 1998, printed pp. 15–16, reproduced “Former Pugilist Shot” clipping and “That Christmas Eve” sequence](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf#page=6).
- Gold says Alexander left Ancker Hospital around midnight on January 21, 1926 in slippers, pajamas, and a robe in below-zero weather. Police suspected he had help from friends in a waiting car. [Gold, printed p. 16, paragraph starting “Around midnight on January 21, 1926”](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf#page=6).
- **Alternate Taran episode if more variety is needed.** Gold quotes the February 23, 1924 *Pioneer Press* report of Taran’s arrest and a large alcohol seizure, then describes warehouse cases labeled as books that officials said held large containers of alcohol. A separate March 7, 1924 Minneapolis seizure also used a books label and had an original contemporary report in a Klan newspaper. The sources concern separate locations and do not establish that the two incidents involved the same shipper. [Gold, p. 14, paragraph with “innocent appearing boxes”](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf#page=4), [*Minnesota Fiery Cross*, March 7, 1924, front page, “Federal Officials Capture Carload of Liquor Billed to Minneapolis Man as ‘Books’”](https://legalectric.org/f/2022/11/Minnsota-Firey-Cross-March-7-1924.pdf).

### Inferences
- The robe and slippers can carry a short standalone scene. The fight’s extraordinary details should retain Gold’s attribution because the clipping printed alongside his story confirms only the attack and its immediate injury. The current two Taran stories address different liquor and finance cases.

### Gaps
- I did not locate a contemporary copy of the hospital-escape report or court transcript beyond Gold’s article. The waiting car is police conjecture in his account. I found no connection between the Minneapolis books seizure and the Saint Paul boxes. The state and city in an unrelated September 1924 story about a still in “St. Paul” are Oregon, so that story is excluded.
