# Saint Paul bootlegging and business cases

## What did agents find behind the steel doors?

### Takeaway
The strongest small episode is the 1925 Wheeler Avenue raid. Agents said Scotch labels and body rub met in a Saint Paul bungalow. The man they charged walked free in that case.

### Cited Findings
- In the June 27, 1925 *St. Paul Daily News*, a caption identifies 817 Wheeler Avenue as the site where federal agents reported finding a redistilling plant behind steel and barred doors. Agents described body rub as the source of its supposed Scotch. Paul R. Gold reproduces the caption, quotes the same day's *Pioneer Press*, and reports the seizure of stills, body rub, fake labels, bottles, stoppers, and capping equipment. The labeled cases included White Horse, Old Parr and Haig and Haig. [Gold, “The Legend of Sam Taran,” *Ramsey County History*, Winter 1998, pp. 15–16, passage starting “His picture and that of his house at 817 North Wheeler Avenue”](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf).
- Gold reports that all but one bottle of the alleged liquor was destroyed by the agents before the October 22, 1925 trial of Sam Taran, known in the papers as the Fighting Tailor or Sammy Terrin. The defense argued that a landlord could be unaware of what took place on the premises. Gold reports that the jury returned a not guilty verdict in twenty minutes. [Gold, pp. 15–16, paragraph starting “Taran’s trial for his operation on Wheeler Avenue”](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf).

### Inferences
- The mismatch between recognizable labels and the contents the agents described can carry a short casebook story. The confiscated bottling equipment and missing bottles give it concrete scenes. The 1927 car paperwork case below could be a second short story or the last paragraph of a profile of Taran.

### Gaps
- I read the quoted newspaper coverage via Gold, who gives dates and outlet names. I did not inspect the original clippings or the federal trial file. Attribute the product and distillery details to the agents through that record. The disappearance of evidence does not establish bribery or who ordered the destruction. Taran’s acquittal rules out presenting him as the proven operator.

## What happened when agents had only a last name?

### Takeaway
The Rogowskis’ dispute went from a bartender’s distinctive little fingernail to a presidential veto. The record preserves both the mistaken identity case and the government’s assessment.

### Cited Findings
- A 1919 revenue report, excerpted in the 1938 Senate committee report on H.R. 4941, quotes an inspector describing the bartender who sold liquor on November 15, 1919. He recorded a yellow-stone pinky ring and a sharply pointed little fingernail, and acknowledged he had heard no first name. Representative Elmer J. Ryan argued that John Rogowski was several inches shorter and roughly 45 pounds heavier than the inspector’s estimate. [75th Congress, S. Rept. 2052, printed June 7, calendar day June 9, 1938, pp. 2–4, especially inspector’s description on p. 3](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-10232_00_00-122-2052-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-10232_00_00-122-2052-0000.pdf#page=3).
- The committee papers record that John pleaded guilty in the wartime Prohibition case and paid a $100 fine. The committee’s account says his attorney advised that this would cost less than fighting the charge. John and Alex paid an additional $1,281.25 in taxes and penalties under protest in March 1920. Witness affidavits say the brothers gave up their South St. Paul saloon and went into men’s furnishings, and suggest that another man, Joseph Rogowski on South Wabasha Street in Saint Paul proper, was the person the inspectors encountered. Ryan’s March 19, 1937 letter to the committee says Joseph sometimes used the name John. It records the agents’ initial allegation as eight glasses at 25 cents apiece. [S. Rept. 2052, pp. 2–4 and 16–18, especially Ryan’s letter](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-10232_00_00-122-2052-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-10232_00_00-122-2052-0000.pdf).
- The Senate committee recommended reimbursement. Franklin D. Roosevelt withheld approval of the same H.R. 4941 in a message dated June 25, 1938. He said their 1921 administrative refund claim was rejected and no suit followed within the statutory time. [*Congressional Record*, pp. 9714–9715, H.R. 4941 message, search “I am withholding my approval of H. R. 4941”](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1938-pt8-v83/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1938-pt8-v83-8-2.pdf), [official bill history recording the veto](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1938-pt12-v83/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1938-pt12-v83-2.pdf), [Minnesota Historical Society finding aid, Elmer J. Ryan congressional files, box 9, H.R. 4941](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/00488.html).

### Inferences
- Open with the ring and the fingernail. The episode is an argument about who sold the drink, followed by the strange life of the resulting tax bill. Identify Saint Paul and South St. Paul as separate places.

### Gaps
- The Senate’s printed record preserves rival claims. John’s plea, the inspector’s description, the recommendation, and the veto have documentary anchors. The report does not establish conclusively that Joseph made the sale or that the case against John was a proven misidentification. The veto is the outcome of H.R. 4941. I did not establish any payment under a separate later measure. This began under wartime Prohibition in 1919.

## What did the paperwork say about the syndicate’s trucks and cars?

### Takeaway
Two court opinions anchor different scenes. In one, a bootlegging crew’s court filings describe a railroad car full of alcohol. In the other, a car buyer goes downstairs to his furnace and hears that his visitor is being guarded.

### Cited Findings
- **The carload and the drugstore corner.** On February 16, 1925, Burton Stevens was shot at the Dreis drugstore at Ninth and St. Peter. Abe and Ben Gleeman were convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to life. In their effort to get a new trial, they submitted a different story: a Twin Cities liquor syndicate with offices in both cities, a previous dispute over a railroad car full of alcohol, and a shipment brought by truck to an Eighth Street garage the morning of the shooting. Ben’s new account blamed Morrie Miller for the killing. The Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed the denial of a new trial. [*State v. Gleeman*, 170 Minn. 197 (1927), ¶¶ 1–2, 8–11, 14–17 and disposition](https://openjurist.org/170/minn/197/state-v-gleeman), [MNHS finding aid for the 1925 testimony and affidavits, entry “Gleeman Case, St. Paul”](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/00530.html).
- Gold separately reports that the syndicate moved whiskey and champagne in trucks marked as carrying lubricating oil and with fictitious company names. He also describes liquor brought in by rail from Cleveland. [Gold, pp. 15–16, “Liquor Hijacking”](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf).
- **The finance papers and the furnace.** In a separate case, the Minnesota Supreme Court upheld Sam Taran’s first-degree grand larceny conviction in January 1929. Prosecutors charged that Taran and auto dealer Mark G. Wynn falsely represented an automobile sale and secured a $700 check for the buyer’s note and mortgage from a Minneapolis finance company. Wynn said Taran and armed associates held him for ten days in February 1927 to force him to forge car contracts and sell the papers. Another witness, a car buyer named Cooke, recalled going down to his furnace during a visit and hearing Wynn say the man accompanying him was there to stop him leaving. Gold places Wynn Motors at 706 University Avenue in Saint Paul. [*State v. Taran*, 176 Minn. 175 (1929), opinion pp. 176–183, find “went to the basement”](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/state-v-taran-minn-1929), [Gold, p. 16](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf).
- **Reserve: the saloon fittings and a new lease.** The 905 West Seventh Street soft drink bar case comes with a different mechanism from Victoria Café: four actual liquor sales and a change of tenant after the violations, followed by a finding that the business and equipment remained in place. In March 1928, the Eighth Circuit upheld a one-year closure ordered in April 1927. [*Engler v. United States*, 25 F.2d 37 (8th Cir. 1928), pp. 38–39](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/25/37/1548515/).

### Inferences
- The Gleeman story can put the railroad car in a passage about what the brothers told the court, alongside the undisputed location and the verdict. Taran’s furnace detail offers a precise, ordinary place where a witness heard a claim about coercion.
- The implemented eleven casebook stories do not include these people or incidents. The existing Cleveland notebook follows a different gang and event. The current Victoria Café story already uses a closure over mixers, making Engler the weaker addition for a short batch.

### Gaps
- The Gleeman opinion expressly distinguishes competing claims from proved facts. The new-trial theory and lubricating oil camouflage should each keep its own attribution. The contemporary case identifies the Dreis drugstore corner, so omit the later landmark shorthand of Mickey’s Diner. Preserve the spelling distinction between the Gleeman brothers and Leon Gleckman from the main tour.
- Cooke’s evidence proves he recalled hearing what Wynn told him about the man. It does not independently verify the man’s origin, his actual job, or every detail of the alleged ten-day detention. The Taran judgment concerns the finance company case and gives no basis to reverse the separate liquor acquittal.
