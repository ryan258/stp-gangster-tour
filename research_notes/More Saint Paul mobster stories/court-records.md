# Saint Paul court and police records

## Which new episodes have a period record behind them?

### Takeaway

The best small incident is the 1936 search for a felony in Harry Sawyer’s past, which produced a fingerprint card, a warning shot and a misdemeanor instead. Two Minnesota Supreme Court opinions give an earlier murder-for-hire case and the unusually well documented aftermath of a home distillery fire. Period reporting on Haskell Bohn’s kidnapping gives a fourth story about three men who sought money from his father while the search was underway.

### Cited Findings

- **The old arrest the prosecutor could not use (1918 and 1936).** In his 1936 federal trial in Saint Paul, Harry Sawyer testified that he had never been convicted of a felony. U.S. Attorney George Sullivan asked investigators to check the Twin Cities records. The January 25 report by Special Agent S. W. Hardy says the Minneapolis police file recorded a September 11, 1918 arrest for attempted grand larceny, under the name Harry Stanley. Detectives Walter Bryant and John Finlayson recalled attempting to catch three people they said were trying to steal a car at Eighth and Nicollet. Bryant fired into the air, according to the detectives’ account. The fingerprint clerk said he could produce the card and photograph plate, Minneapolis police no. 16479. The court record described in the report names the defendant Harry Sandelvoich and shows that he pleaded guilty to the lesser offense of tampering with an automobile. The report reproduces the court entries for a $100 fine or up to 60 days in the workhouse. It records that he paid the fine and obtained his release. Sullivan told the agent the record could not be used for impeachment because it was a misdemeanor. [Bureau of Investigation, Bremer Kidnapping Part 192, S. W. Hardy report dated January 25, 1936, report pp. 1–3, from “On January 21, 1936” through Sullivan’s response](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20192/at_download/file).
- **The burglary that left the diamonds (1917–1918).** Alice McQuillan Dunn was murdered on April 26, 1917, at her family’s house at 793 Selby Avenue. Her husband Frank Dunn was convicted of first-degree murder, sentenced to life, and lost his bid for a new trial at the Minnesota Supreme Court. According to the accomplices’ testimony summarized in the opinion, Mike Moore arranged for the killers to be paid and later complained that they had failed to make it look like a robbery. The opinion says Alice’s diamond rings, watch, and other valuables in the room were left behind, although a small amount of cash was taken from a different room. The accomplices described a late cash payment and a taxi escape that high water diverted from Shakopee to Chaska. The court identified corroboration in Dunn’s admitted visits to a drugstore where accomplices said he and Alice were pointed out, as well as in his visit to a safe-deposit vault on the day of the murder. [State v. Dunn, 140 Minn. 308 (1918), pp. 310–12 for burglary, payment and escape, pp. 315–17 for corroboration, p. 322 for affirmance](https://openjurist.org/140/minn/308/state-v-dunn).
- MNHS identifies Redenbaugh as a Kansas City man who had been involved in the killing of Minneapolis police officer George Connery a day or two earlier. A later Minnesota Supreme Court opinion quotes Redenbaugh’s testimony about intending to take jewelry to make the murder look like a burglary. A 1917 newspaper printed different early payment figures while charges were pending. Use the appellate opinion’s attributed testimony for figures, or omit them. [MNHS notice on the Dunn papers and Supreme Court case files](https://www2.mnhs.org/library/findaids/index-WhatsNew-November2018.htm), [State ex rel. Redenbaugh v. Rigg, 96 N.W.2d 555 (Minn. 1959), discussion of Redenbaugh’s testimony](https://law.justia.com/cases/minnesota/supreme-court/1959/37-580.html), [Oregon Daily Journal, May 18, 1917, p. 2, item on payments in Dunn case](https://oregonnews.uoregon.edu/lccn/sn85042444/1917-05-18/ed-1/seq-2/).
- **The still that became an insurance case (1922 and 1925).** In February 1922 a Saint Paul house sold on installment caught fire. The Minnesota Supreme Court opinion says one of the occupants used an oil stove to warm the room where mash fermented. Escaping oil or a stove explosion started the fire. An insurer cited the illicit still and the dwelling policy to avoid payment. The parties agreed the seller, who had insured the house, did not know about or consent to the distilling. The court affirmed the judgment in her favor and said she could apply the amount against the outstanding purchase balance. The court also said there was no evidence that finished illicit liquor was in the house before or at the time of the fire. [Kierce v. Lumbermen’s Insurance Co. of Philadelphia, 162 Minn. 277 (1925), ¶¶2–8 and 13–16, pp. 278–81](https://openjurist.org/162/minn/277/kierce-v-lumbermens-insurance-co-of-philadelphia).
- **A dummy ransom package for the men who promised to bring Bohn home (1932).** The Associated Press reported that two armed men seized 20-year-old Haskell Bohn near his family’s Saint Paul Summit Avenue home on June 30, 1932. The note handed to the chauffeur demanded $35,000 and, according to Bohn’s father, invoked the recent Lindbergh kidnapping. After Haskell returned, AP reported that police had used a dummy money package to catch three local men who, police said, admitted to trying to get $10,000 by promising his return. His father publicly said no ransom had been paid. In April 1933, the Indianapolis Times separately reported that a grand jury indictment accused Verne Sankey, a person named Robinson, and a John Doe believed to be Gordon Alcorn of extorting $12,000 for his release. The named charge and the alleged dummy-package extortion are separate records. [Daily Iowan, July 1, 1932, printed p. 3, AP story “Youth Seized by Kidnapers for Ransom”](https://dailyiowan.lib.uiowa.edu/DI/1932/di1932-07-01.pdf), [Daily Iowan, July 8, 1932, printed p. 3, AP story “Ransom Not Paid for Bohn,” last paragraph](https://dailyiowan.lib.uiowa.edu/DI/1932/di1932-07-08.pdf), [Indianapolis Times, April 1, 1933, home edition, p. 1, “Bohn Case Arrest”](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn82015313/1933-04-01/ed-2/?st=text).

### Inferences

- The Sawyer document can support a Casebook item about the difference between an arrest, an indictment and a conviction. The passage spans Saint Paul and Minneapolis, with the courthouse search firmly rooted in the federal trial in Saint Paul. The Dunn opinion can support a serious story about how investigators and jurors looked at a purported burglary, and the fire can support a short Prohibition sidebar about the reach of an illegal household business into an ordinary civil case. Bohn works best with the dummy ransom as the focus and the main kidnapping as context.
- The names Dunn, Bohn, Kierce and Soltan and these episodes do not appear in the first eleven story titles. The tour’s Sawyer material concerns the investigators’ account of his preference for kidnapping Bremer over robbing the bank, a different part of the record. ### Gaps

- **Sawyer:** The three sections of the federal report give October 8, 9, and 11 in connection with the old plea and fine. Use “October 1918” for the plea or attribute exact dates to the court entries, which distinguish October 9 for plea and October 11 for payment. The warning shot comes from police recollection recorded in 1936. The same FBI file includes other unadjudicated suspicions about Sawyer, which are unnecessary to this story.
- **Dunn:** The accomplices’ claims about the payment chain were testimony, and the prosecutors’ safe-deposit explanation was an inference, disputed by Dunn. The case has a contract-killer connection and the judicial record establishes the conviction. This does not establish a connection to the O’Connor protection arrangement. Avoid romanticizing the murder or implying the publicly accessible location of the private home.
- **Distillery:** The opinion says the stove leaked oil or exploded and leaves which occupant used it unresolved. Do not give an exact street address or imply a documented supply network or connection to a gang. This is a story about illicit Prohibition production as local context.
- **Bohn:** Both AP reports establish what contemporary readers were told. The AP’s confession claim is attributed to police. The 1933 report describes an indictment, which is an accusation. The father’s denial and the indictment’s alleged payment differ. Skip any definitive payment amount or identification of the three men as the kidnappers without the underlying records. A modern scholarly review of Timothy W. Bjorkman’s book reports $12,000, but the review is not a direct payment record. [Journal of American History review](https://academic.oup.com/jah/article-abstract/95/2/578/708916).
