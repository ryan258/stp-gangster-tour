# Saint Paul court records on protection and closing time

## Ida Dorsey and the price of police protection

### Takeaway

The Casebook hearing follows Ida Dorsey’s move to 151 South Washington Street, the reported testimony about the cost of opening there, and the 1914 bribery convictions of former police chief Martin J. Flanagan and former chief of detectives Fred Turner. Entry `dorsey-price-of-police-protection` uses claims C123–C125 and sources S77–S79.

### Cited Findings

- Dorsey opened a brothel in the former Nina Clifford property at 151 South Washington Street on March 7, 1913. Petersen’s account of the trial says Dorsey testified that she approached Flanagan and offered references from Minneapolis officials. The meeting and references are Dorsey’s reported testimony. [Alexandra Scholten, MNopedia, paragraph beginning with the 1907 enforcement shift and ending with the March 1913 move, S77](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/dorsey-ida-1866-1918), [Penny A. Petersen, *Minneapolis Madams*, p. 167, “Ida Dorsey,” and p. 213, chapter 5 note 29, S78](https://dokumen.pub/minneapolis-madams-the-lost-history-of-prostitution-on-the-riverfront-9780816665235-9780816665242-2013010354-0816665230-9780816665242.html)
- In Petersen’s account, Clifford testified that Dorsey paid her $4,000. Clifford described $1,000 going toward the house and $3,000 intended to secure protection through officials and underworld figures. Petersen suggests the $1,000 could have been a down payment. The chief ordered the brothels closed within months and Dorsey’s business there closed. [Petersen, p. 167 and p. 213, notes 29–30, S78](https://dokumen.pub/minneapolis-madams-the-lost-history-of-prostitution-on-the-riverfront-9780816665235-9780816665242-2013010354-0816665230-9780816665242.html), [MNopedia, next paragraph on the payments and closure, S77](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/dorsey-ida-1866-1918)
- The February 20 Saint Paul dispatch says a district court jury convicted both former officers of bribery the night before and that they received state prison terms of up to one year each. Its description of the charge gives $300 for police protection and leaves the payer unnamed. The headline says “Fined,” while the body specifies prison. [*The Atlanta Constitution*, February 21, 1914, p. 2, “Chief and Detective Fined for Bribery,” S79](https://leofrank.org/library/atlanta-constitution-issues/1914/atlanta-constitution-february-21-1914-saturday-14-pages-combined.pdf#page=2)

### Inferences

- The testimony describes the proposed distribution of a protection payment. The verdict dispatch supplies the outcome for the two officers. The sources leave the relationship between Clifford’s $3,000 figure and the reported $300 charge unresolved.

### Gaps

- The direct trial transcript and sentencing judgment were not located. Petersen’s footnote identifies the *Saint Paul Daily News*, February 11, 1914, “Halt Graft Trial to Probe Charges Against Juror,” and the *Saint Paul Pioneer Press*, February 13, 1914, “Declares She Gave Diamond to Turner.” The complete flow of money and the time either man actually served remain unverified.

## Seven glasses after closing

### Takeaway

The Casebook reconstruction moves from parked cars outside a licensed beer parlor to the officers’ report of seven partly filled glasses in a booth, then to the owners’ explanation and the appellate ruling. Entry `seven-glasses-after-closing` uses claims C135–C137 and source S85.

### Cited Findings

- At 3:15 a.m. Sunday, February 21, 1937, two officers knocked at 419 West Wheelock Parkway after seeing parked cars outside the 3.2 beer parlor. Curtains were drawn and the doors locked. The officers were admitted after a short wait. The city ordinance specified Sunday closing hours of 2 to 5 a.m. [*City of St. Paul v. St. Aubin*, 201 Minn. 208, 275 N.W. 623, pp. 208–209, opening paragraph and Ordinance No. 7641 § 1, S85](https://law.justia.com/cases/minnesota/supreme-court/1937/201-minn-208.html)
- According to the officers’ testimony summarized in the opinion, one man at the bar had a partly filled glass, a man and woman in a booth had seven partly filled beer glasses on their table, and four men in work clothes were hiding in the basement. Others were toward the back. [*St. Aubin*, p. 209, paragraph on what the officers saw after admission, S85](https://law.justia.com/cases/minnesota/supreme-court/1937/201-minn-208.html)
- The owners testified that they stopped the music at closing and allowed people to stay to plan a surprise for the following week, without selling beer during the discussion. The Minnesota Supreme Court held that the officers’ testimony supported the inference that the premises remained open for business, and it affirmed the convictions on November 5, 1937. [*St. Aubin*, pp. 209–210, defendants’ testimony, reasoning under Ordinance No. 7641, and final ruling, S85](https://law.justia.com/cases/minnesota/supreme-court/1937/201-minn-208.html)

### Inferences

- The bar, booth, and basement are discrete places named in testimony and can be inspected as such. The surprise remains the owners’ explanation. The court relied on the inference of after-hours business from the officers’ observations.

### Gaps

- The opinion gives no floor plan, identities for the two people at the booth, or connection to a gang. This is a local licensing and vice enforcement episode.
