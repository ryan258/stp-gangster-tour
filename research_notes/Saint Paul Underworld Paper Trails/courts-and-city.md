# Before the federal gangster files

## What earlier local records can visitors examine?

### Takeaway

Start with the cigar-store offer and the warden’s complaint about an alleged Cole Younger impersonator. Both have original 1901 letters from an archive, and both show how the names of convicted outlaws became part of ordinary Saint Paul business. The 1903 blind pig and 1924 telegraph operator show two distinct encounters with liquor enforcement.

### Cited Findings

- **The cigar counter and the five-cent checks, 1901.** On July 18, state prison agent F. A. Whittier wrote Warden Henry Wolfer about a Saint Paul cigar-shop job proposal for newly paroled Cole and Jim Younger, formerly of the James–Younger Gang. Mr. Dayton’s shop on Wabasha near Seventh had a tobacco cabinet and a cigar counter about fifteen or twenty feet long. Dayton proposed guaranteeing the brothers $75 each per month for a year, with a possible share of profits. Whittier asked about rumors of gambling. His letter describes an openly played card game resembling cinch, in which a player who was set paid ten cents in checks. Dayton sold each check for five cents and redeemed it for five cents in merchandise or bought it back for 2½ cents cash. Dayton maintained it was social play and said the cost discouraged loafers. An undated Globe Hotel advertising card identifies Jim at the Andrew Schoch Grocery cigar department at Seventh and Broadway. Cole’s 1903 memoir says Jim took that job after working for a monument company. [Whittier to Wolfer, July 18, 1901, two sheets, files 699–700, find “his present store located on Wabasha” and “As to the rumor of gambling”](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/gr00206/pdf/gr00206-000003-2.pdf), [Pardon Board files 633–634, Globe Hotel card and files 555–556, July 10 parole record](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/gr00277.html), [Cole Younger, memoir, chapter 34, “On Parole”](https://www.ncows.com/library/pdf/the%20Story%20of%20Cole%20Younger%2C%20by%20Himself.pdf).
- **The salesman using an outlaw’s name, 1901.** In an August 16 letter to Peterson at 104 East Fifth Street, Saint Paul, the warden said he had heard from two sources that a traveling salesman named Bloom was passing himself off as Cole Younger at North Branch, Duluth and elsewhere. He asked Peterson to make him stop. On August 19, P. N. Peterson of the Saint Paul granite company replied on the company’s decorative letterhead that he had just learned of the matter and had written to Bloom, then in Prentice, Wisconsin, telling him to stop. The prison marked Peterson’s letter received August 20. Cole’s memoir records his actual work traveling for the Peterson monument company after parole. [Files 699–700, letters of August 16 and 19, 1901, find “passing himself off” and the Minnesota State Prison receipt stamp](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/gr00206/pdf/gr00206-000003-2.pdf), [Cole Younger, “On Parole”](https://www.ncows.com/library/pdf/the%20Story%20of%20Cole%20Younger%2C%20by%20Himself.pdf).
- **The blind pig before national Prohibition, 1903.** Saint Paul municipal court convicted a man named Stoffels for keeping an illicit liquor room in a locally dry district. One instrument contained the warrants for arrest and search. Officers seized liquor and bar fixtures. The Minnesota Supreme Court upheld the validity of the combined warrant and the admission of the items in evidence. The prosecutor nevertheless cost the state its verdict by twice inviting jurors to infer guilt from Stoffels’s silence. The court held that those remarks violated the law and prejudiced the defendant. It reversed the conviction and granted a new trial. [State v. Stoffels, 89 Minn. 205 (May 8, 1903), pp. 206–207 for the combined warrant and seizure, pp. 210–211 for admissibility, closing argument and reversal](https://openjurist.org/89/minn/205/state-v-stoffels).
- **The telegraph operator’s missed shifts, 1924–1925.** Henry Holland, a Saint Paul public safety telegraph operator, wrote that men representing themselves as federal agents searched his garage while he was working on his automobile. They claimed they found a bottle of liquor. Holland said he was taken to the government building and then jailed, denied a chance to post bail until Monday, and notified his department on Saturday that he could not get out. He denied the liquor charge. Public Safety Commissioner George C. Sudheimer had charged him with unauthorized absence on May 4 and 5 and dismissed him May 27 after finding his explanation unsatisfactory. The Minnesota Supreme Court upheld the employment outcome and did not decide the liquor charge. [State ex rel. Henry Holland v. George C. Sudheimer, 164 Minn. 437, 205 N.W. 369 (Oct. 16, 1925), pp. 438–439, dismissal notice and Holland’s written reply](https://law.justia.com/cases/minnesota/supreme-court/1925/164-minn-437.html).

### Inferences

- The five-cent cigar check, the correspondence about the impersonator, and the folded arrest-and-search paper each give visitors an object to follow. The first two work especially well as a single story about an outlaw’s new line of work and who could profit from his name. Holland’s scene can move between the garage, jail and empty telegraph desk using the places in the record, with his account attributed throughout.
- These scenes and names were checked against the 34 current Casebook entries and the previous three reports. The Youngers are earlier outlaws with a documented Saint Paul connection, while the liquor cases show local enforcement adjacent to the later syndicates. ### Gaps

- **Youngers:** The letter documents a proposed cigar-shop job and Whittier’s description of a game. It supplies no basis for claiming the Youngers dealt cards, took Dayton’s offer, or participated in the layover system. The investigator does not give a criminal finding about the game. Do not recreate the physical design of the checks from the amounts alone. The Bloom story documents the correspondence and Peterson’s reply. The underlying newspaper reports, sales pitch and Bloom’s own account were not retrieved.
- **Stoffels:** The opinion says the complaint described the rooms but does not reproduce their precise location. No retrial result or syndicate connection was located. An illustration can show the two authorizations on one document as an interpretation, without purporting to reproduce the actual warrant.
- **Holland:** The weekend sequence and alleged bottle are from Holland’s reply summarized in court. The appeal does not verify the bottle, the agents’ authority, or disposition of the federal possession charge. This is a Prohibition enforcement story without a proved organized-crime tie.
