# Saint Paul vice and business paper trails

## What was in the driver’s special-delivery envelope?

### Takeaway
In Leon Gleckman’s 1922 liquor case, a special-delivery stamp helped connect a truck driver’s paperwork to a downtown real estate office. The court describes what agents found and why the postmark mattered.

### Cited Findings
- Agents arrested Sol Rosenhause on August 3, 1922 as he drove a truck loaded with redistilled alcohol. From him they took a special-delivery envelope, two bills of lading, a small account book, and a note on Leon Gleckman’s Real Estate & Loans letterhead. The envelope was addressed to Gleckman at 306 Globe Building, Saint Paul, and had arrived the preceding morning. The bills documented freight from Universal Medicine Company in Chicago to Saint Paul’s West Side Pharmacy under the name “Aleo Wash.” The freight referenced in the documents arrived days after the arrest. [*Gleckman v. United States*, 16 F.2d 670 (8th Cir. Dec. 24, 1926), ¶¶ 27–35, printed pp. 674–676](https://openjurist.org/16/f2d/670/gleckman-v-united-states).
- The opinion describes the pharmacy name being used to receive nonbeverage alcohol that was then redistilled and sold for drinking. It says the druggist received 50 cents or $1 a case for the use of his name, while expressing doubt that he knew about the plant. The appeals court inferred that Gleckman received the special delivery and passed it to Rosenhause. Gleckman had pleaded guilty after the government presented its evidence and later sought to withdraw that plea. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the judgment. [*Gleckman*, ¶¶ 24–35 and 51](https://openjurist.org/16/f2d/670/gleckman-v-united-states).

### Inferences
- Put the visitor beside the stopped truck, opening the envelope and reading the postmark, then let them trace the apparent movement from office to driver. This is a distinct case and a paper trail angle, even though redistillation appears elsewhere in the Casebook.

### Gaps
- The passing of the envelope from Gleckman to Rosenhause was the court’s inference. The bill-of-lading shipment was not the load in the stopped truck. The OpenJurist transcription mangles the package noun in the bill. Omit the package count unless a facsimile settles it. Avoid conflating this with Gleckman’s later tax cases or Sam Taran’s Wheeler Avenue raid.

## What did a prisoner send the pardon board?

### Takeaway
A portable camp refrigerator design reportedly accompanied Albert A. Robbins’s bid for commutation while he was serving time for the Saint Paul kidnapping of Leon Gleckman.

### Cited Findings
- A United Press dispatch from Stillwater describes Robbins as serving a 25-year term for his role in the Gleckman kidnapping. It says he began work before sentencing on a portable refrigerator, believed by 1936 that he had perfected a design for hunters, fishermen, motorists and picnickers, and put the plans before the state pardon board in connection with a request for commutation. [*Ranger Times*, July 16, 1936, printed p. 3, “Kidnaper In Prison Works On Invention”](https://newspapers.swco.ttu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/108394a5-9858-4a3d-a4fd-c691ed4b7a5a/content), [same United Press dispatch in *Eastland Telegram*, July 14, 1936, printed p. 4](https://newspapers.swco.ttu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/96888fcf-b636-4559-a7fa-67c7c3f59c12/content).
- While the kidnapping was still unresolved, an October 1, 1931 Saint Paul dispatch described Gleckman’s family waiting for his return and uncertain about reported ransom arrangements. This is useful context if the story starts with the disappearance and jumps five years to the design. [*Bismarck Tribune*, October 1, 1931, p. 1, “Fear for Life of Kidnaping Victim”](https://www.historyworm.com/gazete/bismarck_tribune/1931-10-01/1).

### Inferences
- Start with the unusual pairing of a picnic refrigerator design and a request for a shorter prison term. The detail puts a person with a supporting role in the kidnapping at the center of a scene of his own.

### Gaps
- The two copies are the same wire story. I found no sentencing judgment, pardon board disposition, design drawing, patent or proof that a refrigerator was made. Attribute Robbins’s role and sentence to the dispatch. The archive does not establish that the invention won him release.

## How did Tommy Banks keep his accounts?

### Takeaway
An early book of sales small enough for a vest pocket gives way to railroad freight and an office with no books. Later testimony gives the details, and the court records Banks’s Saint Paul liquor-conspiracy plea.

### Cited Findings
- The Tax Court’s findings say Thomas W. Banks recorded daily liquor sales in a little vest-pocket book from about 1927 through 1929 and kept no record of what the alcohol cost him. It records his November 14, 1929 guilty plea on a liquor-conspiracy charge in federal court in Saint Paul and the $2,500 fine he paid. It also records a separate 1934 conspiracy plea in Minneapolis. [*Banks v. Commissioner*, 20 T.C.M. 1165 (1961), ¶¶ 20–29, especially ¶¶ 23, 27, and 29](https://openjurist.org/20/tcm/1165/banks-v-commissioner-4814361).
- In 1930 or 1931 Banks and others ordered alcohol in boxcars, usually 80 to 100 drums to a car. The court records that he did not keep books for this operation, run out of Minneapolis’s West Hotel. A 1930s federal investigative report, in a separate matter, puts his name in a telephone conversation involving Saint Paul’s Hollyhocks Inn and reports a proposed division of the Minneapolis liquor trade. [*Banks*, ¶¶ 24–26](https://openjurist.org/20/tcm/1165/banks-v-commissioner-4814361), [FBI Vault, Kansas City Massacre Part 13, search “The better liquors are to be controlled by LEON GLECKMAN” and read preceding Hollyhocks passage](https://vault.fbi.gov/Kansas%20City%20Massacre/Kansas%20City%20Massacre%20Part%2013).

### Inferences
- The opening object is a notebook carried in a vest and filled with sales, with the missing expense column as the reveal. The court plea supplies the direct Saint Paul connection. The episode differs from the existing Cleveland notebook about a separate investigation and from the Gleeman railroad-car dispute.

### Gaps
- The business operated on both sides of the metro, and the West Hotel was in Minneapolis. The opinion documenting the period testimony dates to 1961. Its findings do not connect a particular freight shipment to the earlier guilty plea. The federal report preserves claims about dividing the trade and does not establish they were carried out. Avoid folding that claim into the court’s findings.

## Why did the agent go back through the basement window?

### Takeaway
The raid was already over when a Saint Paul Prohibition agent returned to collect fuel and equipment from a South St. Paul still site. The missing key explains the odd route in.

### Cited Findings
- The *Congressional Record* says Wesley A. Frazer and Carl E. Olson investigated 1408 Fifth Avenue South in South St. Paul on December 9, 1927, saw a still and barrels of mash, and brought three men to Saint Paul for questioning. Frazer came back January 2, 1928 with workers from the Star Trading Company to remove seized property. The owner was away and the keys unavailable. The owner’s wife reportedly allowed them in through the basement window and the crew backed a truck up to it to load coke. [*Congressional Record*, June 13, 1929, printed p. 2792, entry “Wesley A. Frazer, prohibition agent, Minnesota”](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1929-pt3-v71/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1929-pt3-v71-9.pdf).
- Both a contemporary Associated Press dispatch and the congressional account report that the owner, Nickolich, fired through the window and fatally wounded Frazer. AP reported an arrest and admission. The congressional account says the coroner’s jury charged first-degree murder. The AP story calls the seized fuel coal and gives the owner’s first name as Gub. The congressional account calls it coke and spells the first name Gulp. [*Daily Iowan*, January 3, 1928, AP article “Butcher, ‘Mad at Dry Agent,’ Kills St. Paul Officer”](https://dailyiowan.lib.uiowa.edu/DI/1928/di1928-01-03.pdf), [*Congressional Record*, p. 2792](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1929-pt3-v71/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1929-pt3-v71-9.pdf).

### Inferences
- The backed-up truck, fuel and basement window are the scene. This is the human cost of enforcing Prohibition and should carry a different tone from the cooler story. The geography is South St. Paul with a direct Saint Paul link through Frazer and the questioning of the suspects. Searches of current Casebook and prior reports found no Frazer, Nickolich, Robbins, Rosenhause, Aleo Wash, or Tommy Banks matches.

### Gaps
- I did not find a court disposition or a verified link to a named gang. The coroner’s account is not a criminal judgment. Given the first-name variation, use Nickolich by surname and refer to the seized material as fuel unless directly attributing a more specific term.
