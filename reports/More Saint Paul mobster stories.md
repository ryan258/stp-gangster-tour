# Saint Paul’s mob cases get stranger

Start with **the Scotch agents said came from body rub**, **the dummy ransom package in the Bohn kidnapping**, and **Harry Sawyer’s old fingerprint file**. The details lead into three different outcomes. A jury acquitted the man charged in the liquor case. Police reported a separate extortion attempt during the search for Haskell Bohn. A federal prosecutor looking for a felony found a misdemeanor. This round adds eleven episodes beyond the current Casebook, including stories whose decisive details survive as testimony or recollection. [Paul R. Gold, *Ramsey County History*, pp. 15–16](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf#page=5), [July 8, 1932 Associated Press dispatch, p. 3](https://dailyiowan.lib.uiowa.edu/DI/1932/di1932-07-08.pdf), [S. W. Hardy report, January 25, 1936, pp. 1–3](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20192/at_download/file).

## The Scotch, the ransom package, and the fingerprint card

**The Scotch that agents said came from body rub.** In June 1925, federal agents reported finding a redistilling plant behind steel and barred doors at a Wheeler Avenue bungalow. They said body rub supplied the alcohol for supposed Scotch. The haul included bottling equipment and cases bearing White Horse, Old Parr, and Haig and Haig labels. Historian Paul R. Gold reproduces a contemporary newspaper caption and reports that agents destroyed all but one bottle before Sam Taran’s October trial. The defense argued that a landlord could be unaware of what went on in the building. The jury acquitted Taran. Use the seized equipment and the agents’ allegation to tell the story, with the acquittal as its outcome. [Gold, “The Legend of Sam Taran,” *Ramsey County History*, Winter 1998, p. 15, Wheeler Avenue caption and trial paragraphs](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf#page=5).

**The dummy ransom package.** Two armed men seized 20-year-old Haskell Bohn near his family’s Summit Avenue home in June 1932. The Associated Press described a $35,000 demand written in pencil on cheap paper. After Bohn returned, police told the AP they had used a dummy package to catch three local men who allegedly tried to obtain $10,000 by promising his return. The report preserves the police account of the trap. The men’s case outcomes remain unresolved. Bohn’s father denied paying ransom, while a later indictment alleged a payment in the kidnapping case. The kidnapping and the alleged attempt to cash in on it are distinct events. [July 1 AP dispatch, *Daily Iowan*, p. 3](https://dailyiowan.lib.uiowa.edu/DI/1932/di1932-07-01.pdf), [July 8 AP dispatch, *Daily Iowan*, p. 3, final paragraph](https://dailyiowan.lib.uiowa.edu/DI/1932/di1932-07-08.pdf), [*Indianapolis Times*, April 1, 1933, p. 1, “Bohn Case Arrest”](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn82015313/1933-04-01/ed-2/?st=text).

**The fingerprint file that led to a misdemeanor.** At Harry Sawyer’s 1936 federal trial in Saint Paul, he testified that he had never been convicted of a felony. The prosecutor had investigators check the old files. The 1918 Minneapolis arrest was filed under Harry Stanley. A fingerprint clerk told the agent he could produce card and photograph plate No. 16479. The court entry used the name Harry Sandelvoich. A detective recalled firing a warning shot during the arrest. The old court entries recorded a guilty plea for tampering with an automobile and a paid $100 fine. The prosecutor told the agent he could not use the record to challenge Sawyer’s testimony because the conviction was a misdemeanor. The agent’s report gives readers the trail through the old files. [S. W. Hardy, January 25, 1936, Bremer Kidnapping Part 192, report pp. 1–3](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20192/at_download/file).

## What turned up in the files after a shooting

**Eddie Green came for the baggage.** Federal agents followed baggage from a Marshall Avenue apartment after the Lincoln Court shootout. They waited at a second location for Eddie Green to collect it and shot him on April 3, 1934. He died in hospital a week later. In an April 10 memo, the Bureau said keys found on Green were ones he told agents belonged to Dillinger. The same memo describes a gunstock that appeared to fit a machine gun left at Lincoln Court. Use the baggage and keys as an aftermath to *The Safe House Fails*. The paperwork gives conflicting addresses for the shooting, and the stock’s apparent fit is the Bureau’s description. [Dillinger Gang Part 12, April 10 memorandum, filing stamp 62-29777-4721](https://vault.fbi.gov/John%20Dillinger%20/john/John%20Dillinger%20Gang%20Part%2012), [August 24 AP dispatch, *Medford Mail Tribune*, p. 1](https://oregonnews.uoregon.edu/lccn/sn97071090/1934-08-24/ed-1/seq-1/ocr/).

**The airline uniform that the FBI said looked like a police uniform.** A federal reconstruction puts three uniformed Northwest Airways workers near Portland Avenue and St. Alban Street in January 1934. The Bureau says gang members mistook the workers for officers following them, opened fire, and badly wounded radio operator Roy McCord. It links the shooting to a delay in the Bremer kidnapping plan. A separate Bureau memo says McCord could not identify Arthur “Doc” Barker as the shooter. A later interview says McCord recovered. The victim’s inability to identify Barker belongs alongside the Bureau’s account of the gang’s motive. [Bremer investigation summary, printed p. 17](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file), [Bremer Part 82, printed p. 111](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%2082), [*Star Tribune* interview with Babe Rohland](https://www.startribune.com/babe-rohland-putting-a-face-on-history/18832684).

## The courts preserved the stories people told under oath

**A railroad car full of alcohol, according to the Gleeman brothers.** Burton Stevens was shot at the Dreis drugstore at Ninth and St. Peter in 1925. Abe and Ben Gleeman were convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to life. In seeking a new trial, the brothers submitted an alternative account involving a Twin Cities liquor syndicate, a previous dispute over a railroad car full of alcohol, and a truck shipment to an Eighth Street garage the morning of the shooting. Ben’s account blamed another man. The Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed denial of a new trial. The carload comes from the brothers’ filing. The verdict and the appeal’s outcome are in the court record. [*State v. Gleeman*, 170 Minn. 197 (1927), ¶¶1–2, 8–11, 14–17 and disposition](https://openjurist.org/170/minn/197/state-v-gleeman), [MNHS finding aid for the Gleeman case testimony and affidavits](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/00530.html).

**What a car buyer heard by his furnace.** Sam Taran’s separate auto-finance case involved a $700 check secured with paperwork for a purported car sale. Auto dealer Mark G. Wynn said armed men had held him for ten days and forced him to forge the contracts. A car buyer named Cooke recalled going down to his furnace during a visit and hearing Wynn say his companion was there to keep him from leaving. The Minnesota Supreme Court upheld Taran’s grand larceny conviction in 1929. Cooke’s account records what Wynn told him. The judgment covers the finance transaction. The liquor jury’s acquittal was in a different case. [*State v. Taran*, 176 Minn. 175 (1929), pp. 176–183, search “went to the basement”](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/state-v-taran-minn-1929), [Gold, p. 16, Wynn Motors on University Avenue](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf#page=6).

**The burglary that left the diamonds.** Alice McQuillan Dunn was murdered at her family’s Selby Avenue home in 1917. Accomplices testified that the murder was arranged for money and was supposed to look like a burglary. Her diamond rings and watch were left behind. Her husband Frank Dunn was convicted of first-degree murder, and the Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed denial of a new trial. The opinion examines how other evidence corroborated the accomplices’ testimony. The case is an earlier contract killing in Saint Paul. The records examined here leave its connection to the police protection arrangement unestablished. [*State v. Dunn*, 140 Minn. 308 (1918), pp. 310–312, 315–317 and 322](https://openjurist.org/140/minn/308/state-v-dunn), [MNHS notice on the Dunn case papers](https://www2.mnhs.org/library/findaids/index-WhatsNew-November2018.htm).

## What witnesses remembered about Carroll and Van Meter

**A patrolman remembered seeing the gun again.** In a 1979 oral history, former Saint Paul officer Herbert Scott said he and his patrol partner confiscated a .45 automatic from Tommy Carroll and took him to the detectives. Scott recalled seeing Carroll on Robert Street about two hours later. He said Carroll opened his coat to show the gun. Scott also said his partner wrote a report that they later could not find. The sequence comes from Scott’s firsthand recollection decades later. The date, the decision to release Carroll, and what became of the report remain open. [Herbert Scott oral history, pp. 17–18](https://spphs.org/oral_history/histories/Scott_Herb_1979.pdf).

**A call about an injured dog and a girl watching from her porch.** Scott recalled responding to a dog struck by a car near University Avenue when he heard gunfire and found Homer Van Meter behind a filling station near Marion and University. In a separate memoir, Bernice Fisher recalled watching officers leave their cars and run up an alley across from her home. Contemporary AP coverage confirms that Van Meter was killed in Saint Paul in August 1934. The dog and porch scenes are later firsthand memories. The order of the shots and exact path of the officers remain unresolved. [Scott oral history, pp. 19–20](https://spphs.org/oral_history/histories/Scott_Herb_1979.pdf), [Bernice Fisher, *Ramsey County History*, Spring 2003, p. 25](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Spring2003_Fisher.pdf), [August 24 AP dispatch, *Medford Mail Tribune*, p. 1](https://oregonnews.uoregon.edu/lccn/sn97071090/1934-08-24/ed-1/seq-1/ocr/).

## A pinky ring went into the congressional record

**The bartender’s fingernail and Roosevelt’s veto.** An inspector who reported a liquor sale in November 1919 noted a yellow-stone pinky ring and a sharply pointed little fingernail. He had heard the bartender’s last name. Representative Elmer J. Ryan’s letter to the Senate committee says the agents initially alleged eight glasses sold at 25 cents apiece. John Rogowski pleaded guilty and paid a $100 fine. John and his brother Alex paid a further $1,281.25 in taxes and penalties under protest. Their bid for reimbursement disputed the inspector’s description and pointed to another Rogowski, Joseph, associated with a South Wabasha Street saloon in Saint Paul. The brothers had operated in South St. Paul. A Senate committee recommended reimbursement, and Franklin D. Roosevelt vetoed the bill in 1938, citing the earlier rejected claim and the lapse of the statutory time to sue. The report preserves both sides of a disputed identification. This is an adjacent wartime Prohibition story. [S. Rept. 2052, pp. 2–4 and 16–18](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-10232_00_00-122-2052-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-10232_00_00-122-2052-0000.pdf), [*Congressional Record*, pp. 9714–9715, veto of H.R. 4941](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1938-pt8-v83/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1938-pt8-v83-8-2.pdf).

**Reserves.** In *Kierce*, an illicit still heated by an oil stove figured in a Saint Paul house fire. The court ruled for the seller who had insured the house and had no knowledge of the distilling. In *Engler*, a West Seventh soft drink bar kept its saloon fittings after a change of tenant, and an appeals court upheld closure after liquor sales at the site. *Kierce* is a distinct domestic Prohibition story. *Engler* came up in the first research round and overlaps with Victoria Café’s closure. [*Kierce v. Lumbermen’s Insurance Co.*, 162 Minn. 277 (1925), pp. 278–281](https://openjurist.org/162/minn/277/kierce-v-lumbermens-insurance-co-of-philadelphia), [*Engler v. United States*, 25 F.2d 37 (8th Cir. 1928), pp. 38–39](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/25/37/1548515/).
