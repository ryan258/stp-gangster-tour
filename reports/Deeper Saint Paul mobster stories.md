# More Saint Paul stories from the records

Three details stand out for the next Casebook additions. Jurors in a police corruption trial wore headsets, and a needle skipped while they listened. Five weeks after William Hamm Jr. was kidnapped, a man the FBI implicated in the abduction reported seeing him on the same flight out of Saint Paul. A Prohibition agent tried a key its owner said was for his car, and it opened a door to a room where agents reported finding moonshine. These are three of twelve episodes absent from the current tour and Casebook. [*State v. Raasch*, pp. 160–161](https://openjurist.org/201/minn/158/state-v-raasch), [FBI memorandum, March 16, 1936](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20206/at_download/file), [*United States v. Weinberg*, pp. 416–417](https://hallapproved.com/mn/cases/federal/district/1923/8847590/).

## The headphones, the disputed call, and the gold

**The jury heard the needle skip.** Saint Paul detective official Fred W. Raasch was convicted of malfeasance for giving gamblers information and advice. The calls had been captured on Cedar 5328, a line that bypassed the police switchboard, using a device the opinion calls a pamograph. Jurors and lawyers listened on headsets. When the needle jumped a groove, the recording was replayed for the missing passage. Typed transcripts helped witnesses and the court follow the calls and were not admitted as exhibits. The Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed denial of a new trial. This adds a courtroom scene to the wiretap investigation already covered in *On the Record*. [*State v. Raasch*, 201 Minn. 158 (1937), pp. 159–162, especially pp. 160–161](https://openjurist.org/201/minn/158/state-v-raasch).

**Charles Tierney wanted to hear the record.** A March letter in the Bremer files describes a federal agent confronting assistant police inspector Charles Tierney about an alleged recorded call. The accusation was that Tierney had told someone to say nothing to Agent John E. Brennan. Tierney denied it and demanded that they go to *Daily News* editor Howard Kahn to hear the recording or get a retraction. Brennan declined. The letter documents the dispute. Whether the recording existed, and what it contained if it did, remain open. [Bremer Kidnapping Part 205, March 14 confidential letter from O. W. Stein, search “Tierney vehemently denied”](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20205/).

**The former police chief’s gold.** A 1936 *Saint Paul Daily News* editorial says former chief Thomas A. Brown acknowledged at a civil service hearing that he had exchanged $900 in gold under a federal gold order and changed a $1,000 bill at a bank. The editorial also says he made loans and used three safe-deposit boxes. The newspaper contrasted these figures with his reported police salary, while acknowledging there could be an explanation. Its account records what the paper reported about the hearing. The source of the money remains unresolved. [*Saint Paul Daily News*, September 24, 1936, “Brown Hearing,” preserved in Bremer Kidnapping Part 267](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20267).

## A passenger manifest and a bus to Rochester

**Hamm and Bolton on the same flight.** On July 23, 1933, five weeks after the kidnapping, Bryan Bolton said he flew out of Saint Paul toward Chicago with Jack Peifer and a woman and saw Hamm and a federal agent aboard. A 1936 memo says Northwest Airways passenger manifests and Agent Coulter’s statement corroborated Bolton’s account. Bolton left the flight at Madison. The FBI later named him among the men it said abducted Hamm, and the press reported that Bolton pleaded guilty. The memo gives no indication that Hamm recognized Bolton or knew he was on the plane. [March 16, 1936 FBI memo, p. 1, “manifest records”](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%20206/at_download/file), [Bremer investigation summary, printed pp. 13–14](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file), [*Daily Iowan*, July 15, 1936, p. 5](https://dailyiowan.lib.uiowa.edu/DI/1936/di1936-07-15.pdf).

**The Shell signs and five flashes.** The FBI’s Bremer summary says ransom courier Walter Magee moved the money into a Chevrolet coupe with Shell signs on University Avenue. Keys and instructions waited in the left front door pocket. Following those instructions, Magee trailed a Rochester bus, watched for red roadside lights, then left two suit boxes by the road after a car flashed its headlights five times. Investigators later took a Shell sign to a sign writer and followed up with the sign painters’ union. The records describe the markings and the investigators’ lead. They leave who made the signs and who operated the lights unresolved. [Bremer investigation summary, printed pp. 23–24 and following scan, “five flashes”](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file), [Bremer Kidnapping Part 29, report before sheet 28, “One of the original Shell signs”](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-kidnapping/Bremer%20Kidnapping%20Part%2029/at_download/file#page=38).

**The wallpaper and the strawberry shortcake.** The FBI’s case summary says Edward Bremer remembered the wallpaper in the room where he was held. Agents found a similar pattern sold by a mail-order house and obtained a specimen, which Bremer said resembled what he had seen. He also described a toilet lever that seemed to be a screw with its enamel broken or missing. One meal stood out in winter. Bremer told agents he had been served fresh strawberry shortcake. The wallpaper specimen was similar, and these details alone do not locate the house. [Bremer investigation summary, printed pp. 24–25, Bremer’s account of captivity](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file#page=25).

## What the agents found behind doors and in bottles

**The car key that opened a room.** In a 1923 federal case, agents testified that a man sold them moonshine at what looked like a grocery, candy and cigar shop on East Thirteenth Street. On a later visit, Weinberg produced two Yale keys and said they were for his car. An agent tried a key in a locked door off the courtyard and it opened. Agents reported finding five-gallon jugs, bottles under a couch and funnels above it. Both defendants disputed the agents’ account. The opinion records guilty verdicts on the counts it discusses and denies the application for a writ of error. This is a local Prohibition case with no established tie to a named gang. [*United States v. Weinberg*, 293 F. 415 (1923), pp. 415–417](https://hallapproved.com/mn/cases/federal/district/1923/8847590/).

**A Squeezor beer bottle sent agents from shop to shop.** Saint Paul police recovered a bottle from a Vernon Avenue address in June 1933. A federal letter quotes a telegram matching its print to Frank Nash’s right thumb. Agents then tried to trace six bottles of Squeezor beer. They showed photographs to shopkeepers and found two neighborhood businesses that carried the brand, but the documented search never identified a seller. When Nash handled the bottle and whether he was at the address remain unresolved. [Kansas City Massacre Part 37, John Hanni’s January 31, 1934 letter, “Latent fingerprints submitted by St Paul Police”](https://vault.fbi.gov/Kansas%20City%20Massacre/Kansas%20City%20Massacre%20Part%2037/at_download/file), [Part 13, internal p. 14, shopkeeper interviews](https://vault.fbi.gov/Kansas%20City%20Massacre/Kansas%20City%20Massacre%20Part%2013).

**Slots on rubber pads and beer on ice.** Police told a court they found about ten cases of beer, some chilled, and two dozen whiskey and highball glasses in Kaasa’s East Eleventh Street apartment. Two slot machines held coins and sat on rubber pads. Kaasa attributed some of the goods to a man in Winnipeg and said he did not know how the machines got there. In 1936 the Minnesota Supreme Court upheld a conviction for keeping liquor for sale without a city license. The opinion says the officers had no warrant and that no actual sale was shown. The record gives an inventory of a post-repeal vice case and a liquor-license conviction. [*State v. Kaasa*, 198 Minn. 181 (1936), pp. 181–184, inventory on pp. 182–183](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/state-v-kaasa-minn-1936).

## A fire hose, hospital slippers, and two thousand mirrors

**Larry De Vol and the fire hose.** A 1932 newspaper dispatch quotes a county attorney saying some of the proceeds from a fatal Minneapolis bank robbery turned up in a Saint Paul apartment connected to Larry De Vol. The FBI’s later summary records that De Vol pleaded guilty to murder and received a life sentence. In 1936, newspapers reported that he helped organize an escape from the St. Peter state hospital. One report described the group prying loose a barred window and using a fire hose to climb down. The papers describe what the group did. Who went down the hose remains unresolved. [*Daily Iowan*, December 21, 1932, p. 5](https://dailyiowan.lib.uiowa.edu/DI/1932/di1932-12-21.pdf), [Bremer investigation summary, printed p. 10](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/at_download/file), [*La Grande Evening Observer*, June 8, 1936, p. 1](https://oregonnews.uoregon.edu/lccn/2006260039/1936-06-08/ed-1/seq-1/), [*Medford Mail Tribune*, June 8, 1936, p. 3](https://oregonnews.uoregon.edu/lccn/sn97071090/1936-06-08/ed-1/seq-3/ocr/).

**Out of Ancker Hospital in slippers.** A period clipping reproduced by historian Paul R. Gold reports that two men attacked former boxer Sam Taran in his Saint Paul apartment. Gold dates it to Christmas Eve 1925 and says the wounded Taran threw one attacker, Billy Alexander, down a staircase. Gold reports that Alexander left Ancker Hospital about a month later wearing slippers, pajamas, and a robe in below-zero weather. A waiting car was the police theory in Gold’s account. The clipping corroborates the attack and Taran’s injury. The staircase and hospital escape come from Gold’s later narrative. [Gold, “The Legend of Sam Taran,” *Ramsey County History*, printed pp. 15–16, clipping and paragraphs beginning “That Christmas Eve” and “Around midnight”](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Winter1998_Gold.pdf#page=6).

**Mystic Caverns advertised two thousand mirrors.** In 1933, the nightclub’s ads promised a ballroom spectacle with two thousand mirrors and directed drivers toward a neon skull and crossbones under the High Bridge. Historian Greg Brick reproduces one ad, identifies co-owner Jack Foster as the Saint Paul police band leader, and attributes the cave’s 1934 closure to a grand jury gambling investigation. A Minnesota Historical Society finding aid lists Robert “Frisco Dutch” Steinhardt as a casino operator at Mystic Caverns. The ads establish what the club promised. The actual mirror count remains unverified. Mystic Caverns was separate from Castle Royal, the cave nightclub already on the tour. [Greg Brick, *Subterranean Twin Cities*, pp. 113–114, illustrated April 14, 1933 ad and adjoining text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386279641_Chapter_Title_THE_MEDIEVAL_TEMPLES_OF_MUSHROOM_VALLEY_ST_PAUL%27S_WEST_SIDE_Book_Title_Subterranean_Twin_Cities), [MNHS Maccabee collection, Steinhardt entry](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/00239.html).
