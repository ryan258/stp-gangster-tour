# Saint Paul’s gangster stories keep going

Start the Casebook expansion with three stories visitors can follow through the evidence. In West St. Paul, a landlady’s son told investigators he recognized two tenants in a detective magazine and that they left their radio playing and dinner on the table. In Frogtown, the Victoria Café’s orchestra made a record that survives, and the café later got padlocked for serving mixers to customers who brought liquor. At the Green Lantern, the court record establishes a murder, and a menu board helped a researcher propose an identification for a set of police photographs. These three episodes add people, places, and texture to the existing chapters. The rest of the slate follows the city’s courtroom, nightlife, and political stories.

## Three Casebook stories to add first

**The tenants left the radio on.** In April 1932, Nick Hannegraf recognized two of his mother’s tenants, Alvin Karpis and Fred Barker, in *True Detective Mysteries*. A Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension report gives the West St. Paul address as 1031 South Robert Street. Hannegraf told investigators the departing tenants left the door open, the gas on, the radio playing, and part of a cooked meal on the table. The FBI’s later reconstruction also records the landlady noticing a violin case. The case’s contents and the identity of anyone who warned the gang are unresolved. Use April 1932 for the episode because the reports differ on the timing and order of events. This fits beside *The Arrangement* as a story about the people living next to fugitives. [Minnesota BCA, Case 1075, April 30, 1932, pp. 4–7](https://vault.fbi.gov/Kansas%20City%20Massacre/Kansas%20City%20Massacre%20Part%2048/), [FBI Bremer investigation summary, printed pp. 5–6](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/), [MNopedia’s Barker–Karpis history](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/barker-karpis-gang).

**The café that got padlocked over ginger ale.** Frank Cloutier and the Victoria Café Orchestra recorded “Moonshiner’s Dance” in 1927 at the Lowry Hotel, and Smithsonian Folkways has the recording. In March 1928, a federal judge ordered the Frogtown café closed for six months after agents reported staff serving ginger ale and ice to patrons who brought their own liquor. In a separate December raid, former assistant county attorney Allan McGill punched a federal agent and said he thought the café was being held up. Researcher Kurt Gegenhuber reproduces the next-day newspaper front page and McGill’s telegram. This is a Prohibition nightlife story for *Under the Bluff*, with the recording available at the source and McGill’s explanation attributed to him. [Smithsonian Folkways recording](https://folkways.si.edu/frank-cloutier-and-the-victoria-cafe-orchestra/moonshiners-dance-part-one/african-american-music-american-folk-blues-old-time/music/track/smithsonian), [Gegenhuber, pp. 146–151 and 162–165](https://www.kurtgegenhuber.com/uploads/1/3/0/5/130566057/gegenhuber_moonshiner_essay.pdf), [Saint Paul heritage designation](https://stpaul.legistar.com/LegislationDetail.aspx?FullText=1&GUID=7951FF83-0FF8-48B7-A988-2C847D227B24&ID=846363).

**The Green Lantern and the pork chop sandwich.** In March 1931, John Quinn shot Frank Ventress behind the café. A jury convicted Quinn of second-degree murder and the Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed the conviction. In 2019, the *Pioneer Press* reported how a researcher tried to identify police photographs using a synagogue roof ornament, three booths, and a menu board advertising spaghetti and a pork chop sandwich. Several photos bear the same case mark, but one exterior view is dated May 1930. The court record supports the account of the shooting and verdict. The photographs’ identification remains open, and their use as trial exhibits has yet to be established. This fits beside *The Arrangement*, which already names the Green Lantern. [State v. Quinn, 186 Minn. 242, pp. 243–244 and 249–253](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/state-v-quinn-minn-1932), [1934 new-trial ruling, pp. 88–90](https://calculators.law/caselaw/decisions/0y48qy7bE8zx/state-v-quinn), [2019 reporting and photographs, reproduced](https://twincitiesmusichighlights.net/venue/green-lantern-i/).

## Four more stories from the case files

**Edna Murray sawed through the bars.** The FBI’s 1936 summary records Murray’s 1925 Kansas City robbery conviction and three escapes from Missouri State Penitentiary. In December 1932, she and Irene McCann escaped by sawing through their cell bars. Murray later faced federal charges in the Bremer case in Saint Paul. Those charges were dismissed in May 1935 and she returned to Missouri to serve time for the robbery and escape. A 1925 newspaper establishes that the press used the nickname “Kiss-Me.” Whether she kissed a robbery victim remains unverified. [FBI Bremer investigation summary, printed pp. 10–11 and 44–46](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/), [Nowata Daily Star, October 2, 1925, p. 1](https://gateway.okhistory.org/ark:/67531/metadc1320191/m1/1/).

**A Cleveland notebook led back to South St. Paul.** The 1933 Swift payroll robbery outside the South St. Paul post office killed officer Leo Pavlak and wounded another officer. Investigators later found a notebook in a Cleveland raid. The FBI said a route in it began at Stockyards National Bank and identified it as the getaway route. That identification is the investigators’ conclusion. The chart itself has yet to be located for this story. [Minnesota House account of the Pavlak commemoration](https://www.house.mn.gov/Caucus/View/DFL/35747), [FBI memorandum, May 12, 1936, p. 5](https://vault.fbi.gov/Eliot%20Ness/Eliot%20Ness%20Part%2001/at_download/file), [FBI Bremer investigation summary, printed p. 14](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/).

**Finger surgery and a print on a gasoline can.** The FBI’s investigation summary says three men, including Arthur “Doc” Barker, underwent facial and fingertip surgery near Toledo. It says their fingers hurt so badly that companions, including Edna Murray, had to feed them. The summary also records that a can bearing Barker’s print was introduced over a defense objection at the Saint Paul trial. The surgery detail comes from the FBI’s reconstruction. The can gives the Casebook a separate evidence item alongside the existing account of fingerprints on the Hamm ransom letters. [FBI Bremer investigation summary, printed pp. 33 and 45](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/), [MNopedia’s Barker–Karpis history](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/barker-karpis-gang).

**The FBI says it used a necktie.** An FBI retrospective says the agents who arrested Alvin Karpis in New Orleans in 1936 had arrived without handcuffs, so an agent used his necktie to bind Karpis’s hands. Contemporary coverage confirms the arrest and Karpis’s return to Saint Paul. He pleaded guilty in the Hamm case. The tie remains an FBI account, suitable as an attributed coda. [FBI capture history](https://www.fbi.gov/news/stories/a-byte-out-of-history), [The Daily Mail, May 2, 1936, p. 1](https://newspaperarchive.com/the-daily-mail-may-02-1936-p-1/), [FBI Bremer investigation summary, printed p. 54](https://vault.fbi.gov/barker-karpis-gang/bremer-investigation-summary/Bremer%20Investigation%20Summary%20Part%2001%20%28Final%29/).

## Four ways into the city behind the gangsters

**The mayoral wager had a death clause.** A surviving 1908 note on Merchants Hotel stationery records a $1,000 stake from Richard T. O’Connor, the police chief’s brother, against $600 from industrialist Philip W. Herzog. The bet was void if either bettor or either candidate died before the election. The note is addressed to Pierce Butler, later a U.S. Supreme Court justice. The payout and Butler’s role remain open. [Minnesota History, spring 2026, pp. 21–23, receipt MNHS P1190-27 on p. 23](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/70/v70i01p018-028.pdf).

**The Science Museum dig found Nina Clifford’s brothel.** A 1997 excavation uncovered the foundations of Clifford’s establishment at 147 South Washington Street. Differences in food remains and tableware gave archaeologists a way to study life on the site. The excavators found no tunnel to the Minnesota Club in the area they examined. The material record gives this story a focus on the people who lived and worked there. [Washington Street excavation summary](https://www.fromsitetostory.org/tcm/21ra0032wsrd/21ra0032wsrd.asp), [Historical Archaeology, 39(1), pp. 74–88](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF03376678), [MNopedia biography of Nina Clifford](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/clifford-nina-1851-1929).

**Dinner at the Hollyhocks Inn.** In an October 1934 interview, proprietor Jack Peifer told federal agents that escaped prisoner Frank Nash and his wife had eaten at his Saint Paul club shortly before Nash was killed in the Kansas City Massacre. The FBI summary records Peifer’s account of dinner. An Associated Press dispatch separately records his 30-year Hamm kidnapping sentence and his death the same day. The FBI interview and the Hamm conviction give the club a link to both cases. Accounts disagree on the details of Peifer’s last hours. [FBI interview summary, Part 66, search for “On October 11, 1934 JACK PFEIFFER”](https://vault.fbi.gov/Kansas%20City%20Massacre/Kansas%20City%20Massacre%20Part%2066), [Associated Press, July 31, 1936, p. 1](https://oregonnews.uoregon.edu/lccn/sn90066132/1936-07-31/ed-1/seq-1/), [Minnesota Historical Society finding aid, Hollyhocks file in box 5](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-finding-aids-public/library/findaids/00239.html).

**The florist’s extra faucet.** Helen Warren Pfleger, a clerk and switchboard operator in the Saint Paul Prohibition Office, recalled in a 1975 memoir that an agent turned a faucet behind a flower shop’s fern stand and got liquor. This is a secondhand recollection from someone who worked in the office. The shop, date, and enforcement outcome remain unidentified. It fits as a brief attributed anecdote in a story about how agents and their office worked. [Ramsey County History, Spring/Summer 1975, pp. 19–20](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Spring-Summer1975_Pfleger.pdf).

## How the Casebook can use the records

Give each first story one thing a visitor can inspect at its source, such as the investigator’s report, the recording, or the court opinion. The detail that makes the visitor curious can also tell them what kind of evidence they are looking at. Hannegraf’s radio is in an investigator’s account of what he said. The Victoria Café recording survives as audio. The Quinn verdict and the later photo identification come from different kinds of records. Link the source beside the claim and leave the photographs for provenance review before reproducing them. The local [evidence ledger](../docs/evidence-ledger.md) describes the project’s review process.
